### The Vigil of the Descent. Year 304 of the Pilgrimage.

They took the light away a little at a time, the way it had been done every year of Wren’s life and every year before it.

The Vigil was kept in the hub, where there was no weight. The first Descent had been made by three people sitting still in a small space while everything outside it pressed inward, and the hub was the nearest the ship could come to that. Three thousand people did not fit. The ones who could be spared from a watch tethered themselves in rings around the wall, family by family, and the rest listened from the arms. The shutters were drawn over the great window. For one hour of the year you could not see the stars from the heart of the ship. The old people said this was the hardest part and the children said it was the best.

First the white lamps went and the chapel was blue. Then the blue thinned to quarter power, which the Reader called the memory of blue. Then that went too, and there was only breathing, and the small sounds that tethers make.

It lasted as long as the first one had lasted. Nobody was permitted to make the Descent shorter, though Wren knew for a fact that people had asked.

She was fifteen, and she had been named for the vessel. There were two other Wrens in her year, and four Natashas, and so many boys called Hess that the catechists told them apart by their fathers. Her own small brother was a Hess. He was six and had been told he must not speak, and she could feel him not speaking against her arm.

She had been taught that a wren was a kind of bird, and also a kind of boat, and that before either of those it had been a man who built churches. She had never seen a bird. She had never seen a boat. She had lived her whole life inside a church.

What she liked about the Vigil, and had never told anyone, was that for one hour of the year nobody could see her face.

The Reader was a Hess as well, a loud boy two years ahead of her, chosen for the loudness. His voice came out of the dark at the appointed depths.

“At eleven hundred meters Natasha spoke of the warmth of the water, and the Founder kept it in her heart.”

“And the water was dark,” said the rings.

“At sixteen hundred meters the snow rose past them, though nothing was falling.”

“And the water was dark.”

“At two thousand meters the Silent One turned the vessel toward the place. And he said: Twenty minutes. And it was more than he was used to give.”

People smiled at that. You could not see them do it, but the dark changed. It was one of four places in the year’s liturgy where you were allowed to find something funny, and Wren had always thought it strange that the joke was about a man who did not talk.

Then the long part, the description of the place, which was the Founder’s own account and was given slowly: the rock that was only rock, and then the rock that had been asked to make room. Wren knew it the way she knew the walk from her family’s quarters to the gardens. She let it go by.

“And at the bottom of the sea the Founder looked upon the place, and for a long time she only looked. And then she said—”

The whole hub said it with him. Wren said it too. Nobody could see a face in the dark, but anybody could hear a gap.

“We need to call the surface.”

### North Atlantic, 2075. Two thousand meters.

Hess had the channel open before she finished asking for it.

The Wren spoke to the ship by sound. Nothing else worked through two kilometers of water. Light gave out in the first few hundred meters and radio long before that, so a voice went up as pressure, at the speed pressure travels in cold seawater, and took a second and a third to arrive. The answer took a second and a third to come down. Miriam had found this maddening on her first dive. By her fourth she had learned to say a whole thing, stop, and wait, the way you would dictate a letter to someone in another room.

“Surface, Wren.”

She counted it. Up. Somebody’s hand finding a handset. Down.

“Wren, surface. Go ahead.”

Iyer. The link flattened every voice to the same gray, but nobody else on the ship said go ahead as though it were a concession. Iyer was chief scientist, and the two-hundred-thousand-year figure was hers. She had run the argon herself, and run it again, and then spent two dinners and half a night defending it against colleagues who preferred to believe in dirty samples. Dunmore, the petrologist, had been the worst. He had a way of saying intrusion from an older stratum that made it sound like a diagnosis of the person he was talking to.

Natasha was holding the handset and had not yet spoken into it.

Miriam understood the hesitation. It was Natasha’s dive. It was Natasha’s site, too, a geological feature in a geological survey, right up until somebody called the thing in the rock an artifact. After that, by the paragraph in the charter that Iyer had recited to her at three that morning, it became Miriam’s.

Natasha held the handset out to her.

It was a generous thing to do, and a correct one. Miriam took it, and noticed that this was the last moment at which the object in the rock had belonged to no one.

“We’re holding station over the void,” she said. “It isn’t a gas pocket. Regular ovoid, uniform walls, smooth inside. There is an object at the center of it. Cylindrical, about a meter long. Natasha is sending you the rendering.”

The lag went by, and then a good deal more than the lag.

“Say again your last.”

She said it again, in the same words and the same order. Locke had taught her that. When you repeat a finding, repeat it exactly, or people will hear the difference and decide you are less sure.

The rendering went up the way the voices did, a few thousand bits a second. Hess used the time. He walked the Wren along the margin of the fine-grained rock, a meter off the bottom, and the floods showed what the sonar had not. The fine rock was a slab. It had an edge, and the edge ran straight for four meters and turned a corner.

“That’s wrong,” Natasha said, mostly to herself. She had her face close to the port. “The grain’s wrong. Fine grain is fast cooling. You get it at the skin of a flow, where the lava meets the water and quenches. But quenched basalt is a mess. It’s glass and cracks. This is fine all the way across and there isn’t a crack in it.” She sat back. “Fast and even. You don’t get both.”

“You get both if you’re in charge of it,” Miriam said.

Natasha did not answer that. She wrote something down.

At the north-east corner of the slab the ordinary basalt beside it had fractured, the way ordinary basalt does, and taken a bite out of the slab’s edge as it went. The gap was about the width of a wrist. The lights did not reach the bottom of it.

So there was her crack. They had spent an hour looking for one and found it at the one place she had not thought to look, at the edge of the thing and not over it. Miriam felt something close to gratitude, and then less. It was a wrist wide. The object on the sonar was a meter long.

“Wren, stop sending.”

It was Iyer, and it was not her dinner-table voice. Natasha’s hand moved. The upload bar stopped at sixty percent.

“Hess. Are you hearing a chirp down there?”

Hess reached up without a word and switched something on. A speaker in the overhead began to tick. It was a small dry sound, like a fingernail on a tabletop, and it came once every four seconds.

“Twelve kilohertz,” he said. “Somebody’s mapper. I’ve had it ten minutes. I took it for one of ours.”

“It isn’t ours. It’s nine kilometers north of you and working this way.”

Miriam looked at Hess.

“The link’s not a wire,” he said. He seemed almost apologetic about it. “It’s a loudspeaker. Anything out there with a hydrophone gets your picture at the same rate the ship does.”

The North Atlantic was full of other people’s machines. There were cable layers down here, and nodule harvesters, and the survey drones of a dozen countries and a hundred firms, all mapping the same ground for the same metals and none of them obliged to say hello. It had never occurred to her to think of them as an audience.

“I want you up,” Iyer said. “Quietly. I want this planned with everyone in the room.”

It was the correct instinct, and Miriam shared it. Locke used to tell first-years that excavation is the orderly destruction of a site. You only find a thing where it lies once, and everything after the first touch is a report on what used to be there. He had said it to her directly, in her second year, with the weary emphasis he kept for students he expected to be careless. She had never been careless. She had sometimes wondered whether that was what he held against her.

But she could do sums as well as Iyer could.

“If we come up, it is eighteen hours before anything goes back down,” she said. “In eighteen hours that mapper will have flown a grid over this site. It will see what we saw. A hollow with flat walls. And someone will send something to look.”

She let the lag go by and did not fill it.

“The lid is broken at one corner. An old natural fracture, already open to the water. I can have a camera inside in ten minutes. We look, we record on board, we send nothing. Nothing touches anything. Then at least you’ll know what it is you’re planning for.”

This time the silence was very long. She could imagine the comms shack. It held four people comfortably and there would be nine in it.

“Look,” Iyer said. “Don’t touch. Record everything, send nothing. Past that it’s your call, Dr. Calder. It’s what we brought you for.” And then, in a different tone: “Get behind something. If that thing paints you sitting on top of the site, we have told it where to dig.”

Hess had not turned around.

“It’s my call first,” he said.

They both looked at him. It was the longest sentence either of them had heard him volunteer.

“That arm goes into a hole I can’t see into. The rule is you don’t put a manipulator anywhere you can’t see all of it. Whatever it catches on, you are now attached to.” He said it without emphasis, as he might have read out a depth. “I’ll do it. But if it fouls, I don’t work the problem. I blow the shoulder and we leave the arm behind. We agree that now. Not then.”

“Agreed,” Natasha said.

“Agreed,” said Miriam, and meant it.

“One more thing. To get the angle on that corner, and to get out of that mapper’s way, I have to set us down behind the ridge. When I do, we lose the ship.”

“For how long?”

“Until I bring us back up.”

### The Vigil.

After the call came the Pause.

Everyone learned it before they learned to read. The Reader spoke the Founder’s words, and the people kept silent for one breath and a third of a breath, and then they gave the surface’s reply. Small children practiced it on their fingers. If anyone came in early, the catechist stopped the room and began again from the top, and you learned quickly not to be the reason.

“Surface, Wren,” said the Hess in the dark.

One breath, and a third.

“Wren, surface,” said everyone she had ever met. “Go ahead.”

It was strange to hear your own name said by three thousand people who did not mean you. The other Wrens said they had got used to it. She had not.

Nobody had ever been able to tell her why the Pause was that long. She had asked when she was small and been told that it was the length of the Pause. She had asked again at twelve, a different catechist, and been told that the Founder had waited, and so they waited, and that the waiting was a kind of trust. That was a better answer. She had noticed that it still wasn’t one.

She had an idea of her own, which she kept. She thought it sounded like someone waiting to find out whether anyone was there.

There were five calls before the ridge, and five replies. Wren had decided beforehand, the way you decide to hold your breath to see how long you can, that at the fourth reply she would say nothing. She was not trying to mean anything by it. She only wanted to find out what it was like to be in the hub and not be part of the sound.

“And the Founder said: Nothing touches anything.”

One breath, and a third.

“Look. Do not touch,” said the rings. “Past that it is yours.”

Wren kept her mouth closed.

It was like nothing. The reply went by without her and was exactly as loud. Her brother’s arm was warm against hers. She felt, briefly, a great relief, and then a disappointment she had not expected, as though she had knocked on a wall to see if it was hollow and it was simply a wall.

Then, from the ring behind and above her, where the catechists tethered, she heard one person’s breathing change.

She gave the fifth reply very clearly.

“And the Silent One set the vessel down in the shadow of the ridge,” said the Reader. “And the ridge came between them, and the surface was heard no more.”

There was no reply to that. There was the Silence, which was different from the Pause. Nobody had ever timed it. It lasted until the Reader chose to go on, and Readers were remembered for how long they dared. Three thousand people hung in the dark with nothing to say and no one to say it to, and you could hear, if you listened, how hard all of them were listening.

### Two thousand meters.

The link went the way Hess had said it would. He slid the Wren sideways and down into the lee of a wall of basalt the height of a house, and the ticking in the overhead grew faint and stopped. So did the ship. There had been a hiss of carrier on the surface channel for four hours, so constant that Miriam had stopped hearing it. Now she heard it end.

Hess read the board aloud, which he did not usually do. She understood that he was putting it on the recorder.

“Battery sixty-four percent. Oxygen ninety-one hours. Scrubber good. Outside temperature three degrees. Depth two thousand and eleven.” He paused. “Floods off.”

“We need light,” Natasha said.

“You need light in the hole. The wand has its own. Out here I’d sooner not be the only bright thing on the ridge.”

He put them out.

Miriam had thought she knew what dark was. She had sat through the descent, the blue and then the memory of blue and then nothing. But after that there had always been the floods, a white room that the Wren carried around with her. Now there was instrument glow on three faces, and the ports had stopped being windows. They were black disks, and behind each of them was the whole of the sea.

It was three degrees outside the hull and not much more than ten inside it. The wall of the sphere ran with condensation, their own breath coming back to them, and every so often a drop let go and landed on a sleeve. She was wearing two pairs of socks. The scrubber fan made a sound like someone thinking.

Hess unshipped the wand. It was two meters of jointed arm with a light, a camera and the spectrometer’s optical head at its tip, and it had been built for looking down the throats of hydrothermal vents. He fed it through the gap a joint at a time, by feel.

The monitor came alive.

It was the only light. She was aware of that before she was aware of what it showed. The three of them were sitting in the dark looking at a bright gray rectangle, and the brightness was coming from inside the chamber. For two kilometers in every direction, the one lit place in the sea was the place they had come to look at.

The underside of the lid came first. The sonar had not flattered it. It was smooth enough that the light seemed to slide. Miriam looked for tool marks the way you look for a signature, patiently, across the whole of the frame, because every made surface she had ever seen carried some trace of how it was made. She found none. It had the finish a river stone has, of a thing arrived at by a process and not by a hand.

Then the floor of the chamber, and on the floor, silt: a thin even drift, carried in through the break over however many thousand years the break had been there. That was as it should be. In the deep, everything is slowly being buried. Marine snow falls on wrecks and cables and the bones of whales, and given a century it softens every edge there is.

Then the cylinder.

It was gray, the gray of wet slate, and it lay along the long axis of the chamber on three low ridges of the same fine rock. So that was how it stayed centered. The rock had been asked to hold it as well.

It was not large. The sonar had not prepared her for that. A meter long and about as thick as a thigh: something one person could carry in both arms. Every important object she had ever handled had been smaller than its photographs. She noticed the part of herself that wanted this one to be bigger, and set it aside.

Nobody in the cabin said anything. It occurred to her that she was one of three living things that had ever seen it, and that the other two were waiting for her to tell them what it was.

“It’s clean,” she said.

Natasha looked up from the tablet.

“There’s silt on the floor. There’s none on that. No crust, no film, nothing growing.” Put a steel plate down here and within a season it has a skin of bacteria, and then things that eat the bacteria, and then things with shells. “Nothing at this depth stays clean unless it is doing something to stay clean.”

“A current through the break would sweep it.”

“It hasn’t swept the floor.”

Natasha made a note, which was how you knew she agreed.

Hess, unasked, took the camera slowly along it from one end to the other, and then back along the far side.

There was nothing on it. No seam, no cap, no join. No mark of any kind.

Miriam looked for one with the whole of her attention, as she had looked at the lid, and for a better reason. People who make a thing and put it somewhere on purpose nearly always write on it. A name. A number. A warning. In thirty years she had never handled a deliberate deposit that said nothing at all.

This said nothing at all. It occurred to her that she was, for the moment, of no professional use to anyone, and that it was an odd thing to feel relieved about.

But the place said something, if the object did not. You could not find it by accident. A net would not find it and a diver could not reach it. To know it was here you needed an instrument that could see a hollow inside solid rock, and to look at it you needed a vessel that could carry eyes down through two hundred atmospheres and bring them home. If you wanted a thing never found, you did not give its room walls that straight. If you wanted it found soon, you did not put it here. Someone had set the price of looking at a certain level of competence, and then waited for somebody who could pay.

It was not hidden. It was addressed.

She kept that behind her teeth. It was not a finding yet. It was the shape a finding would have if it turned out to be true.

“Every centimeter of it,” she said. “Overlapping frames, two light angles. As far round as you can get without touching the cradle.”

“That’s four minutes,” Hess said.

“Then it’s four minutes.”

He began. Natasha put the laser on the cylinder amidships, well clear of the band, a green dot the size of a lentil. Miriam knew the principle the way she knew how an engine worked. You shine one pure color at a thing. Nearly all of it comes back unchanged. A very little comes back shifted, by an amount that depends on what bonds it met on the way. Read the shifts and you have a list of what the thing is made of, and you have not laid a finger on it.

Natasha held the dot there for ninety seconds while the counts built. She read the result. She moved the dot and ran it again. Ninety seconds. Then she was quiet in a way that was different from her usual quiet.

“It’s a phosphate,” she said. “The mineral. Nothing the library will put a name to. And there’s organic signal under it. Bound in, not a film. You said yourself there’s no film.” She turned the tablet so Miriam could see two low humps that meant nothing to her. “Those are amide bands. That’s protein. Mineral laid down on a protein frame.” She stopped. “I have seen this kind of spectrum before, and it was bone.”

“It isn’t bone.”

“No. It’s the same idea as bone. Shell, tooth, bone. Something living lays down a scaffold and the mineral grows onto it. Nobody carved that, Miriam. It grew.”

For the first time in five dives she saw Hess’s hands stop moving.

“Jesus,” Natasha said, very quietly, to no one.

Miriam found that she had nothing to put in that place.

She had retired the whole vocabulary twenty years ago, deliberately and with care, the way you clear a dead relative’s house. It had not been a slow leak. She had gone through it room by room. She had read the people who defended it, in the languages they wrote in, and found in each of them the exact sentence where the argument asked her to look away. She had finished, and closed the file, and built a life on the far side of it that was honest and well lit and did not require her to address anyone who was not in the room.

In twenty years she had not once reached for one of those words and found the shelf empty. She noticed it now. She noted it as a fact about herself, as she would have noted a reading out of range, and turned back to the monitor.

“Do the cradle,” she said. “I want to know whether the rock is rock.”

Ninety seconds.

“Basalt. Just basalt.”

So the rock was rock, and the thing lying on it had grown.

The speaker in the overhead ticked.

It was one tick, very faint. All three of them looked up at it, as though it were possible to see a sound. Four seconds later it ticked again, a little less faint.

“It’s come round the end of the ridge,” Hess said. He had the passive display up, a waterfall of green noise with one bright thread in it. “It’s flying a lawnmower. East to west, step south, back again. Two more lines and we’re in its swath.” He looked at the monitor, at the gray thing lying in its light. “So is that.”

Nobody said the next thing, which was that there was nothing whatever they could do about it. You cannot hide a hollow from sound.

“I’m bringing the arm out.”

“You’ve done half of it.”

“I’ve done what I’ve done.”

“Hess. If someone else gets here before we get back, the frames on this boat may be the only record there ever is of it where it lay. One more minute.”

He looked at the waterfall, and at the battery, and at her. Then he took the camera round the far side of the cylinder, faster than he had taken the near side, and she watched the gray go by. When the minute was up he said, “That’s it,” and began folding the wand back toward the gap.

He had it most of the way out when the Wren rang.

It was a small sound. One knock, carried up the arm and into the sphere, and she felt it in her teeth. On the monitor the picture went from gray to white.

“What was that?”

Hess was reading a number. “Something’s come down on the arm. Second joint.”

He tried the wrist. He tried the elbow. He gave the Wren a breath of thruster, and she moved a few centimeters on her skids and stopped, like a dog reaching the end of a lead.

“A piece of the lip,” Natasha said. “That edge is all cooling fractures. We must have nudged one loose going in.”

He pulled once more, gently. The number went up and stayed up.

“It’s seating.” Then, for the recorder: “Arm fouled at joint two. Four hundred newtons and holding. Vehicle is restrained.”

He had told her about this on her first day aboard, in the safety brief, in about twenty words. People who have never been down think the danger is the pressure. It isn’t. The sphere had been holding back two hundred atmospheres all morning and would go on doing it until it rusted. The danger is being held. A submersible is safe for exactly as long as it can leave. Take that away and everything else aboard turns into a clock: ninety-one hours of oxygen, sixty percent of a battery, and a ship two kilometers overhead with no second vehicle and no way of reaching down.

The speaker ticked. It was not faint any more.

Hess’s left hand went to a guarded switch beside his knee and lifted the guard.

“Dropping the arm.”

“If you drop it,” Miriam said, “where does the end go?”

He did not answer. He knew that she knew. The arm would part at the shoulder and the Wren would float free. The pinned joint would become a pivot, with a meter of titanium and a camera housing hanging beyond it, inside the chamber, in white water. It would swing down. There was one thing in that chamber to land on, and that thing was made like bone.

“Dr. Calder. We agreed.”

“Wait.”

She put three people on one side of it. She put a gray object a meter long on the other. She did this quite coolly, as she did everything, and she waited for the scale to drop on the side of the three people, as it obviously must.

It did not drop at once.

She watched that happen in herself with something very like professional interest. It was not a reading she had expected from the instrument. And she had the strong sense that if she went on looking at it she would learn something she could not afterward not know.

The speaker ticked.

There was something else in the picture. She turned to it, with relief.

The gap they had come in through was a fracture, and fractures in cooling basalt are not slots. They flare. This one had been wrist-wide at the lip and wider below. She had watched its walls fall away from the camera on the way in and thought nothing of it. A loose block in a flaring crack is a wedge. Hess had the arm in tension. Each time he pulled, he dragged a wedge toward the narrow end of the crack that held it.

It was what she had been trained to see, long before the machines. Not what a thing is. How it came to be lying where it lies.

She went through it once more from the lip down, slowly, because she was about to stake the object on it.

The speaker ticked.

“You’re pulling it tighter,” she said. “It’s a wedge. The crack opens downward. Don’t pull. Push.”

“Push in?”

“Thirty centimeters. The block drops past the joint and falls clear. It’s over open floor at that corner, not the cradle.”

“I can’t see thirty centimeters. I can’t see one.”

“You fed it in by feel. You can feel them.”

“Miriam,” Natasha said, in a voice that asked several questions at once.

“If I push and you’re wrong,” Hess said, “I put the head through that thing.”

“If you push and I’m wrong, you’ve lost five seconds and you drop the arm anyway. Dropping it is the last thing you can do. It will still be the last thing you can do in five seconds.” She heard how reasonable she sounded. She had no idea whether she was. “But if you drop it now, it lands on the only thing down here that matters. And you will never know whether you had to.”

Hess looked at the switch under his thumb.

She had no authority here. It was his boat. He had stated his rule beforehand, and she had agreed to it in front of a witness and a recorder. All she had was that she had spoken as though she were certain.

He closed the guard.

He pushed.

The number fell to nothing. Somewhere below them something knocked twice on rock, going down, the second knock softer than the first. And at the very end of the stroke, with the picture still white, a different number came up on Natasha’s tablet. It was a small number. It held for half a second and went away.

“Contact,” Natasha said. “Head of the wand. A little under two newtons.”

Nobody breathed.

“It gave,” Hess said. He was already drawing the arm back, and this time it came, all of it, joint after joint out of the gap. “Whatever I hit. It gave and came back. Stone doesn’t do that.”

The wand folded against the hull with a sound like a knuckle.

“Arm clear. Vehicle is free.” He let out a breath. “Lifting.”

“Hess. I told you where that block would fall. I have to know where it fell.”

He pointed at the overhead without looking. The speaker ticked, obligingly.

“Thirty seconds,” she said. “Camera at the lip. Not through it.”

He did not answer. But he put the wand out again, as far as the lip and not a centimeter beyond, and aimed it down into the white.

The white became gray. The gray developed a grain, and the grain slowly became a floor.

The block was the size of a large book. It lay in a ring of disturbed silt, a hand’s width from the nearest ridge of the cradle.

The cylinder was where it had been. There was no mark on it where the wand had struck. There was no silt on it, either. The cloud had settled on everything in that chamber except the one thing in the middle.

“A hand’s width,” Natasha said.

“Yes.”

“You read the crack. I’ll give you the crack. Nobody could have read the last ten centimeters.”

“No.”

“So you bet them.”

“Yes,” Miriam said.

“Lifting,” said Hess, and this time nobody argued.

### The Vigil.

The Hess ended the Silence sooner than Wren would have. She thought he had lost his nerve.

“And in the dark the Founder looked upon the vessel of the Word, and saw that nothing of the sea had settled on it, and that nothing was written on it.”

“And it kept its silence,” said the rings.

“And a stone fell from the lip of the place and held the vessel fast. And the Silent One would have loosed the arm and let it fall. And the Founder said—”

“Wait,” said three thousand people, softly.

“And she told him where the stone would fall, and it fell where she had said. And the Founder put forth her hand, and laid it upon the vessel of the Word, and it was warm, as a living thing is warm. And she knew.”

“And she knew,” said the dark.

Wren said it. She had said every reply clearly since the fourth.

In the schoolroom there was a copy of the Notebook under glass, open to the day, and every child on the ship had been stood in front of it. The page for the Descent had a time written at the top and three short lines beneath. There was nothing on it about a hand. There was nothing about warm. The catechists said that she had been too moved to write more, and that the Vigil remembered what the page could not.

Wren had accepted this at seven, and again at eleven. At fifteen she had stood at the glass and thought that it looked like the handwriting of someone being careful.

The light came back the way it had gone. The memory of blue, then blue, then white, and then the shutters drew off the great window and the stars were where they had been left. People cried at that. People always did. Her brother said, in his ordinary voice, “Can I talk now,” and their mother laughed and said he could, and all around the rings three thousand people were unclipping and stretching and beginning to be hungry.

There was bread afterward, from the ship’s own wheat. It was the one night of the year the bakers did not ration it.

Sister Perpetua found her in the queue.

She was sixty or so, a small exact woman who had taught Wren her letters and half the people in the queue theirs. She did not take Wren out of the line. She only said, “Walk out along the arm with me when you’ve eaten,” and moved on, touching a shoulder here and there. Wren stood with the bread in her hand and could not afterward remember having eaten it.

Going out along the transept, the weight came back into your feet a little at a time. Children raced it, hub to rim, to feel themselves get heavy. The sister took it at an old woman’s pace with one hand on the rail. People had written prayers on every wall of the arm. There were three hundred years of them, layered, the oldest worn to gray smears under the newest. Wren had read those walls all her life without reading them.

“You were quiet at the fourth,” Sister Perpetua said.

“I lost my place.”

“No, you didn’t.”

It was not said unkindly. It was said the way she used to correct a letter made backwards. They walked a little farther.

“I did it the year I was sixteen,” the sister said. “The second reply. I had planned it for weeks.”

Wren looked at her.

“What happened?”

“Nothing happened. That was what frightened me. I had thought I would feel free, or be struck down, one or the other. It went by exactly the same without me.” She walked for a while. “They made me Reader the next year.”

“As a punishment?”

“As a cure. It is very hard to hear a gap in your own voice when it is the only voice.” She smiled slightly. “It works, you know. I don’t say that bitterly. I have been glad of it most of my life.”

They had come to a place where the arm widened and there was a bench under a strip of the gardens’ light. The sister sat. Wren stayed standing, because she had not been told to sit.

“Your name is going forward for next year. I wanted you to hear it from me.”

So that was what it was. Wren understood that she was being given something, and that it was the thing people on the ship most wanted for their children. She understood that it was also a door being closed very gently, with her on the inside of it. She understood that the sister knew she understood. Nobody had raised a voice. Nobody would.

She heard herself say, “Sister, why are there only three lines?”

“In the Notebook.”

“Yes.”

“Because she was too moved to write more.”

“It doesn’t look moved,” Wren said. “It looks careful.”

Sister Perpetua was quiet for long enough that Wren began, without meaning to, to count it in breaths.

“Yes,” the sister said. “I have always thought so too.”

She stood, with a hand on the bench, and straightened her habit.

“That is a thing you may think. A great many of us think it. You would be surprised who. It is not a thing that can be said from the Reader’s place, and you will find, when you are standing there, that you don’t want to say it. The people in the rings are holding on to each other in the dark. You will hear it. It is the most beautiful sound on this ship, and the whole of it is people who are not sure, saying the replies very clearly so that the person beside them can be.”

She touched Wren’s shoulder, as she had touched the shoulders in the queue.

“A breath and a third,” she said. “Don’t rush the Pause. Everyone rushes it their first year.”

### Rising.

He took them up out of the shadow of the ridge, and the ship came back all at once, in the middle of a word.

“—ren, surface, respond. Wren, surface, respond.”

It was Iyer, and from the cadence of it she had been saying it for a long time.

“Surface, Wren,” Miriam said. “We’re clear and we’re intact.”

The lag. She had never been so glad of anything as she was of that second and a third of nothing with a human being at the far end of it.

“Wren. Thank God. Report. Keep it short.”

She chose her words for a loudspeaker. “We looked. It is what it appeared to be. Everything is recorded on board. There was one contact with it. Incidental. Force logged.”

The lag.

“Define incidental.”

“We hit it with the camera.”

That silence went on for some time.

“Nothing more on this channel,” Iyer said. “Come up.”

Hess dropped the weights. There were two of them, steel, a quarter of a tonne each, and they fell away into the dark without a sound. The Wren began to rise. It was the one thing she did that needed no power. She was simply lighter than the sea now, and the sea put her out.

It was seventy minutes to the surface. There was nothing to do in them.

“What was on the other side?” Natasha said.

She said it without turning her head. In the instrument glow her face was a few planes of green.

“The other side of what?”

“You stopped. Before you worked out the wedge. You went somewhere for a couple of seconds, and then you came back and did the geology. I’ve sat next to you for a month. I know what you look like when you’re thinking, and that wasn’t it.”

It did not occur to Miriam not to tell her. For twenty years her work had been finding out what was true and then saying it to someone, and she had never had a reason to learn another way.

“I was weighing it,” she said. “The three of us, against that.”

“And?”

“And it took longer than it should have.”

Natasha absorbed this.

“How much longer?”

“I found the wedge before I had to find out.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“No. It is what happened, though.”

She had expected that saying it would make it smaller. That was how it had always worked. You took the thing you had found and you put it in front of another person, and then it was in the room and not only in you. She watched Natasha’s face and saw her take it in as information. Natasha believed her. Natasha was not shocked. Natasha simply had not been where Miriam had been for those two seconds, and could not go there by being told.

It was a small thing. She did not make much of it. But she was aware that she had handed something across and that it had not arrived.

“I’d have dropped it,” Hess said.

Both of them looked at him.

“The arm. I had the guard up. I’d have dropped it and gone home and never thought about it again, and that thing would be in two pieces under a meter of titanium.” He kept his eyes on the depth. “I’m only saying. Whatever it was you were doing for those two seconds, I wasn’t doing it at all.”

Nobody had anything to add to that.

Miriam took the notebook out of her chest pocket. Her hands were not quite steady, and she waited until they were. She found the page with the question on it, the one from four days ago, written in the mess over cold coffee while the geologists argued. You date the ship, not the seafloor. When did it arrive? Under it, in last night’s hand: No way in. Look for the cut.

She wrote the answer under both. The rock came after.

Lava leaves the ridge at eleven or twelve hundred degrees, and it does not go around things. It buries them. This lava had met a gray cylinder a meter long and had made a room for it, and cooled fast, and cooled evenly, and left it lying clean on three small ridges. So either they had come before the rock and known the rock was coming, or they had told the rock when to come. She did not know which was stranger. She found that she did not have to decide today.

She turned to a clean page and wrote the time. Under it, three lines.

Grown, not made.

Nothing settles on it.

It gives.

She looked at the first line a moment longer than the others. It had come out in a cadence she knew from somewhere, an old one, with a bell in it. She did not go looking for where.

Then she held the notebook out to Natasha, because that was what you did with a finding.

Natasha read the three lines, and read them again.

“That’s all?”

“That’s what I know.”

“It isn’t all that happened.”

“No,” Miriam said. “The rest isn’t a finding.”

Natasha gave the notebook back. Outside the port, so gradually that neither of them could have said when it began, the black was turning into the memory of blue.

### The Vigil. After.

Wren did not go back to her family’s quarters when the bread was finished.

The schoolroom was in the port arm, a third of the way out, where the weight was enough to keep a stylus on a desk and not enough to tire small children. It was empty on feast night. Only the case at the front was lit, as it was always lit, and she went and stood in front of it the way she had been stood in front of it at seven.

It was not the Notebook. Everyone knew that. The Notebook itself was in the hub, behind the altar, in a reliquary opened once in a generation. This was a likeness, exact down to the stain on one corner where the Founder had put it down on something wet.

The page was smaller than she remembered. It was always smaller than she remembered.

Grown, not made.

Nothing settles on it.

It gives.

The catechists taught that the first line was the proof. The Founder had been an unbeliever for twenty years; the whole ship knew that, it was the heart of the story. And yet in the very hour, before she had read a single sign, the old words had risen in her. Begotten, not made. She had reached for the Creed without knowing she reached. What more did anyone want?

It could be that. Wren could see that it could be that.

It could also be that a woman had looked at a thing that had grown and had not been made, and had written down what she saw in the fewest words, because she was cold and tired and careful. And the words had fallen that way because she had once known the others very well.

Wren turned the two of them over. She found, to her surprise, that she did not need one of them to win. It was the first thought she had ever had about the Founder that was neither for her nor against her, and there was nobody on the ship she could tell. There was Sister Perpetua. But the sister had already told her what could be said, and from where.

Far off down the arm somebody was singing one of the feast songs, badly.

“Surface, Wren,” she said aloud.

Her own voice in the empty room embarrassed her. She waited anyway. One breath. A third of a breath.

Nobody answered. She had not expected anyone to.

What she had not expected was that the waiting would be the part that felt true. Not the call, and not the reply. The breath and a third in between, when you had said your whole thing and stopped, and it was on its way, and you did not yet know.

She said it again, more quietly, and this time she did not rush it.
