On the ships they have five ways of telling it.

The hardline faithful say she was a prophet, called by God, who did what prophets have always done. The moderates say she was a brilliant woman who was shown something true and let her hurry ruin how she carried it. The skeptics, who do not say it loudly, say she was the most successful political operator who ever lived and that she had the luck to find a useful object. The mystics say it makes no difference what she believed, because God used her either way.

There is a fifth account. Nobody likes it and it will not go away. It says that she was entirely sincere, and entirely correct, and that what she did was wrong all the same.

They agree on very little else. They agree on the number of the dead, which is a number with nine zeros in it. They agree that the ships exist because of her, and are where they are, three centuries out from a world none of them has seen, falling toward the middle of the galaxy to meet a God she told them was there. And they agree, because it is in the one record that no party disputes, that on the morning it began Miriam Calder was forty-six years old, had been an atheist for twenty of them, and could not get a cup of coffee.

### North Atlantic, 2075. 02:20.

The urn in the galley was full and hot and had a note taped to it in Natasha’s handwriting. The note said MIRIAM, NO.

She stood in front of it for a while anyway.

It was a fair note. The dive was at six. After that it was six and a half hours in a sphere two meters across, with two other people and no plumbing of any kind, and you learned on your first descent to stop drinking the evening before. Miriam had learned it on her first descent. She had resented it on each of the four since. She had slept well every night for a month, which was not like her, and tonight she had not slept at all, which was, and what she wanted more than anything the North Atlantic could offer was something hot in a cup.

She went and looked at the sea instead. It was black and moving and had no opinion.

On her way back she saw that the light was on in the dry lab.

Iyer was at the mass spectrometer. She was the chief scientist, a small upright woman of fifty-eight who kept her reading glasses on a cord and pushed them up into her hair when she meant to argue. They were in her hair now, though there was nobody in the room to argue with. She had her hands tucked into her sleeves against the cold and her eyes on a screen where a row of small blue squares was building, left to right, one every ninety seconds.

“You’re diving in three hours,” Iyer said, without turning round.

“I know.”

“Then you should be asleep.”

“So should you.”

“I’m not diving. I’m not allowed.” She touched her left ear, briefly, as someone else might touch wood. “Sit down if you’re staying. Don’t breathe on anything.”

Miriam sat. She had been aboard four weeks as the expedition’s cultural analyst, a post the charter required and nobody wanted, and in that time she had been in this room perhaps twice. It was not her kind of laboratory. Her instruments were models and her samples were text. But she knew what a person looked like who was running something for the third time in the middle of the night, because she had been that person, and she knew it was not a moment to be told to go to bed.

“Which run is this?”

“The fourth.” Iyer almost smiled. “I told Alistair three. Fresh chips this time. A different piece of the core, from forty centimeters down, cut with a clean saw. If something’s been getting into my samples, it won’t have got into these.”

“And the squares?”

“Each one’s an age.” She freed a hand to point. “You heat the sample a little, you measure the argon that comes off. You heat it a little more. Each step gives you a date. If the rock is honest, every step gives you the same date and the squares sit in a flat line. We call it a plateau. If the rock has been got at, older argon carried in from somewhere, you don’t get a line. You get a staircase, or a saddle. The steps disagree with each other.”

Miriam looked at the screen. There were eleven squares. They sat in a line you could have laid a ruler on.

“How many more?”

“Four.”

They watched the twelfth one arrive. It took its place beside the others without any fuss.

Miriam had heard the number for the first time at dinner, two nights ago, and had enjoyed it enormously. It had gone round the table with the energy that scientists generate when something doesn’t make sense, or when they are given the chance to call out a colleague for idiocy. It was not excitement exactly. It was more like the agitation of a system encountering an input it can’t process. The basalt around the object at the dig site had come back at one hundred thousand years, and then, on a better sample, at two hundred. Nobody at the table had been able to leave it alone.

She did not yet know what the object was. Nobody did. It was a shape on a sonar profile, under rock.

“He’s not wrong to ask, you know,” Iyer said. “That’s what is so annoying. Contamination is the likeliest thing. If a student brought me this number I would say exactly what he says.”

“You wouldn’t say it like that.”

“No.” The thirteenth square. “No, I would not say it like that.”

The door opened and Dunmore came in sideways, which was how he came through most of the doors on the ship.

He was the petrologist: sixty-one, very large, with a new hip and a voice that carried through bulkheads. He had a mug of tea in one hand and a clipboard under his arm, and he stopped when he saw the two of them, with the expression of a man who has walked into the wrong lavatory.

“I saw the light,” he said.

“You saw the light from the petrology bench,” said Iyer, “which is where you have been since midnight, looking at thin sections of my basalt to see whether it’s been altered. I can hear the saw through the wall, Alistair.”

“I was making tea.”

“Has it been altered?”

Dunmore considered the tea.

“Kamala,” he said, “two hundred thousand years ago there was barely an us. The cleverest object on this planet was a stone with an edge on it. And you would like me to believe that something is sitting under rock of that age, in a hole, on purpose.”

“I haven’t asked you to believe anything. I’ve told you how old the rock is.”

“Then the rock has been got at.”

“By what?”

“An older stratum. Silts from below. Mineral intrusion that’s skewed your ratios. It happens, it’s mundane, it’s fixable, and it is a great deal likelier than the thing you are being so careful not to say.”

“It plateaus.”

“On the same core.”

“On a different piece of the same core.”

“Which went through the same suitcase.” He meant the neutron source, which lived behind a second door at the back of the lab and was the reason the room had a radiation placard. Every sample had to spend two days in it before it could be dated at all. “If your flux monitor is off, every age you get is off by the same amount, and it will plateau beautifully at the wrong number.”

“The monitor is a sanidine standard that has been dated in forty laboratories.”

“Then I expect it’s something else.”

The fourteenth square arrived. It was on the line.

Miriam was not following the argument for its geology. She could not have. She was following it for what she could follow, which was the words, and she had been doing that for two nights now with growing interest.

Iyer never rounded. That was the first thing. People who are unsure of a figure soften it without knowing they are doing it: about, roughly, on the order of. At dinner everyone had said *two hundred thousand*. Iyer had said, every time, *two hundred and four, plus or minus eleven*, the way you would give your own address to a taxi.

And Dunmore, who was very sure indeed, had never once said the number at all. In two nights of argument it had not been in his mouth. He said *your figure*. He said *what you are not saying*. He said *the thing*.

You could learn a good deal about what people believed from the words they would not use. It was most of what she had been paid for, these last ten years.

“That’s fifteen,” Iyer said.

Nobody spoke. The instrument ticked as it cooled. On the screen the software drew a line through the squares, thought about it, and printed a number underneath.

“Two hundred and three,” said Iyer. “Plus or minus nine.” She took her glasses out of her hair and put them on, and looked at it properly. “Well. It has come down by a thousand years. You must be very pleased.”

Dunmore said nothing. He drank some of his tea.

It was then that Miriam saw the clipboard.

He had shifted it to free his hand, and for a moment the top sheet faced her. It was covered in pencil, small and neat, columns of it: a man working something through by hand because he did not trust the machine or the woman who ran it. At the bottom of the last column he had drawn a box. Inside the box he had written a number, and then gone over it twice so that it stood out dark.

*201.*

He saw her see it. He turned the clipboard over against his chest, without hurry, and looked at her with a kind of weary dislike, as if she had read his post.

So he had run it himself. Tonight, by hand, from her raw counts, and he had got what she got. He would not say the number. But he had written it down.

“Go to bed, Alistair,” Iyer said, not unkindly.

“I intend to.” He got himself turned round in the doorway. “It will be something dull, you know. It always is. I have been asked to look at forty-one marvels in my life and every one of them was a mistake or a liar.” He paused. “I’d put money on the mistake. I don’t think you’re a liar.”

“Thank you.”

“It wasn’t a compliment. Liars are easier.”

He went out sideways. They listened to him go down the passage, slowly, favoring the hip.

“He checked it,” Miriam said.

“Of course he checked it. He’s a pig, but he’s a scientist.” Iyer took her glasses off again and rubbed the bridge of her nose. “What did he get?”

“Two hundred and one.”

“He showed you?”

“He forgot to hide it.”

Iyer laughed, once, a short dry sound that seemed to surprise her. Then she sat quite still for a moment with her hands in her sleeves, looking at the fifteen squares.

“Eight years,” she said. “I had a number once that nobody would have. It took eight years. I was right, and it was the worst time of my life, and I swore I would never again be the only person in a room who believed a thing.” She glanced at Miriam. “I don’t know why I’m telling you. You’re the only one aboard who doesn’t have a view.”

“I don’t know enough to have a view.”

“That has never stopped anyone on this ship.”

There was a knock on the open door.

It was the sponsors’ man. Carrow. He was forty or so, of medium height, and in four weeks Miriam had not once managed to remember what his face looked like when he was not in front of her. He ate early and alone and had usually finished before the scientists sat down. She had taken him for an accountant. He was fully dressed, at half past two in the morning, and did not look as though he had been to bed.

“Dr. Iyer. I’m sorry to disturb you. I heard voices.”

“We’re finished. Is something wrong?”

“No.” He did not come in. “That figure. The date. Has it gone ashore?”

“It’s in the daily report.”

“Which is sent when?”

“Six. With the dive notification.”

“Hold the date out of it, would you? Until I’ve read the report myself.” He said it pleasantly, as he might have asked her to pass something. “The dive notification can go as usual.”

Iyer looked at him. “It’s a provisional number. It’s of no interest to anyone but six geologists.”

“Then it will keep. Thank you. Good night, Dr. Calder.”

He was gone. He had made no sound arriving and he made none leaving.

“What on earth,” said Iyer.

Miriam did not answer at once. She was thinking about dinner, two nights ago. Carrow had been on his way out with his tray when Iyer first said the number aloud. He had stopped. It had been for about as long as it takes a person to decide not to stop, and then he had gone on, and she had thought nothing of it.

An accountant would not have known which number to stop for.

“I don’t think he’s an accountant,” she said.

“He’s the consortium’s representative. He counts things.”

“He asked you one question, and it was whether the information had left the ship. He didn’t ask whether it was right.”

Iyer thought about that. It was plain that she did not like it and equally plain that it was not her field, and Miriam watched her set it down and step away from it, the way a tidy person steps away from a mess in someone else’s kitchen.

“I’ll hold the date,” she said. “It costs nothing. It’s in the contract that he can ask.” She began shutting the instrument down, one valve at a time, in an order she clearly did not have to think about. “May I ask you something? I mean it kindly.”

“Go on.”

“What are you *for*?”

Miriam laughed.

“The charter says cultural analyst. Fine. Every survey carries one, like a fire extinguisher. I’ve had eleven. I had a man who identified a Liberty ship from its galley china. I had a woman who wrote forty pages on a shipping container of dolls. They were archaeologists, or near enough, and they were glad of the money.” She closed a valve. “You are not glad of the money. When I asked the agency who they were sending, I got one paragraph, and a lawyer had written it. And you take your notes on *paper*. So. What do you do, when you are not on my ship classifying telegraph cable?”

It was a reasonable question, and it had been a long time since anyone had asked it who was permitted to hear most of the answer.

“I read things nobody can read,” Miriam said.

“Codes.”

“Sometimes, lately. It didn’t start there. It started with dead scripts. You have a few thousand broken tablets in a language with no descendants. Nobody alive knows a word of it. I build a model that looks at the marks and tells you what kind of system they are, and after a while, if there is enough of it, what some of it says.”

“A machine translates it.”

“No. A machine counts. It tells me how many different signs there are and which ones turn up next to which. The reading is still a person.” She found she was enjoying herself. She had not been allowed to explain her work to a stranger in years. “Then some people noticed that the same method works on other material. Traffic. Things said in ways that are meant not to be understood. They pay better than museums. They also don’t let you bring in anything that remembers, which is where the paper comes from. I got used to it.”

“And now?”

“And now I’ve finished a piece of work for them, and when you finish a piece of work for them there are ninety days in which you may not do anything of the kind for anyone. It is supposed to let the work cool.” She shrugged. “I was going to spend it reading novels. Then a request came for a cultural analyst on a geological survey, with a name on it that I knew. So I’m classifying your telegraph cable.”

Iyer had stopped with her hand on the last valve.

“Dead scripts and codes,” she said. “It’s the same job?”

“It’s the same two questions. Is this a message or is it noise? And if it’s a message, what are its rules?”

“And which is it, usually?”

“Noise,” Miriam said. “Almost always. Scratches. Weathering. A potter’s tally. Somebody’s radio with a fault in it. The hard part of the work was never the reading. It’s that the people who bring you a thing want it to be a message so badly. They have already decided. And you have to stand in front of them and say, I’m sorry, there is nobody there.”

“How do they take it?”

“They find someone else to ask.”

Iyer closed the last valve. The instrument sighed and was quiet.

“I know that job,” she said. “I’ve only ever had to do it to rocks.”

They sat for a moment in the hum of the room. It was companionable. Miriam had not expected that, and she noticed, with some amusement, that it was the first conversation she had had in a month in which neither party wanted anything.

“Two hundred thousand years,” she said. She said it rounded, deliberately, and saw Iyer notice. “Do you know what the oldest writing is? That anyone has found?”

“Tell me.”

“A little over five thousand. Clay, from what is now Iraq. Lists of grain and sheep. I have spent my whole life at the far edge of what can be read, and your rock is forty times farther out than that edge.” She shook her head. “There is nothing for someone like me at two hundred thousand years. There can’t be. Nobody was keeping records. There was nobody to keep them.”

“Then you’ll have a restful dive.”

“I expect so.”

“If there is something made down there,” Iyer said, and stopped, and began again more carefully. “If the object turns out to be an object. In that sense. The charter is quite clear. It stops being a geological feature and becomes an artifact, and an artifact is yours. Your call, I mean. Not mine and not Natasha’s.”

“I’ve read the charter.”

“I thought you might have.” She stood, and winced, and flexed her fingers. “I only mention it because I should not like to find out at two thousand meters that you didn’t want it.”

“It will be a rock, Kamala.”

“Yes,” said Iyer. “I expect it will.”

She turned out the light over the bench. In the dark the screen was still up, and the fifteen squares sat on it in their line, as level as the horizon.

### 03:10.

She did not go to bed. She went to her cabin and sat on the bunk with her boots on and brought up the sonar.

The survey bot had made another pass over the site at midnight, lower and slower than the last, and the new profile had been sitting on the ship’s net for three hours with nobody looking at it. It was the same picture as before, a little sharper. A cross-section of the sea floor, in false color, like a slice through a cake. On top, a thin dusting of sediment. Under that the basalt, a single thick flow, dark and uniform. Under the basalt an older surface, lumpy and pale, which was what the bottom had been before the lava came.

And in the basalt, low down, close to that older surface, the object. A small hard bright return about a meter long. It had been bothering her for days.

It was not the date that bothered her. The date was the geologists’ problem and they were welcome to it. Dunmore said the number was wrong. Iyer said the number was right. They had been at it for two nights, and what struck Miriam was that they were having the same argument from opposite ends. Both of them took it for granted that the age of the rock had something to tell you about the thing inside it.

It did not. Not by itself. Any first-year could have told them that, and twenty-eight years ago she had been one.

You don’t date a shipwreck by dating the seafloor around it. You date the ship. The seafloor may be ten million years old and the ship went down in 1943. What you ask about a find is not how old its surroundings are. You ask when it arrived, and how it got in.

She had come to scripts by way of the ground. Before the models, before the rooms with no devices, there had been four years of trowels and section drawings under a man who taught her to read a layer of dirt the way other people read a face: automatically, without deciding to. Things lie where they lie for reasons. A coin under a floor is older than the floor, unless somebody dug a hole. A pot in a ditch tells you about the ditch only if you can show the ditch was not cut later. The whole discipline came down to one suspicious habit. Before you believe the layer, look for the cut.

So there were two ways for a bright object to be inside a lava flow.

The first was that it had been there when the lava came. It was lying on the old sea floor, and the flow went over it, and it was sealed in. In that case it was at least as old as the rock. Two hundred thousand years, and Iyer could have her plateau.

The second was that it had got in afterward. Basalt cracks as it cools. Fissures open and stay open. A thing dropped from a ship, or lost off a cable, or shed by one of the ten thousand machines that worked this ridge, could fall into a crack and lodge at the bottom and be buried by a century of silt. In that case the rock was two hundred thousand years old and the object was a transponder that somebody had lost in 2061.

That was the dull answer. It was the right kind of dull. It needed no contamination, no faulty standard and no marvel, and it made Iyer and Dunmore both correct, which neither of them would enjoy. Miriam had been fairly sure of it since the first profile. She had not said so at dinner, because it was only a guess and she did not hand guesses to people who were already shouting.

She had only wanted to see the crack. With a crack she could write her report: modern debris, intrusive, of no cultural interest. It would be her fourth, and her shortest.

She enlarged the new profile and went over it slowly, from the surface down.

The sediment lay flat and unbroken. Beneath it the top of the flow was continuous from one edge of the image to the other. She followed it across twice. There were fractures in the basalt, plenty of them, fine bright threads where the rock had split as it cooled. None of them came anywhere near the object. Above it, and for several meters to either side, the flow was as solid as a wall.

There was no cut. Nothing led in.

She sat back.

It meant very little. The resolution was poor. A crack a hand wide would not show at this range, and tomorrow, in the lights, she would very likely find it in the first ten minutes and feel foolish. She told herself so, and she believed it. It was by a wide margin the likeliest thing.

But she took the notebook out of the pocket of her jacket and turned to the page from four days ago, where she had written one line and shown it to no one.

*You date the ship, not the seafloor. When did it arrive?*

Under it she wrote: *No way in. Look for the cut.*

She had learned when to hand over a finding and when to sit on it from the same man who had taught her about ditches, though he would have hated to know it. It was a rule about evidence and nothing else. A guess was not a finding. Once it was a finding, you told people.

His name was Locke. He had taught her as an undergraduate, and he had made an unnecessarily clear show of disliking her, for reasons she had never fully understood. She had been good. She had been careful. He had marked her as though carefulness were a kind of impertinence. And then, the year she was twenty-six, when she had been out of the field for some time and was not sure she would ever go back to anything, his name had appeared on the recommendation that put her on the Antikythera team.

The mechanism itself had come up from the wreck in 1901: a lump of green bronze that turned out, half a century later, to be a geared computer, two thousand years old. But the wreck had gone on giving. In the fifties a storm shifted the boulder field that had been sitting on the rest of the ship since before Caesar, and under it were three new fragments of the back plate, covered in writing nobody had seen.

Nobody could see it then, either. The bronze had gone to something like chalk. The letters were a millimeter and a half tall and lay under the surface, and you read them by putting the fragment in a scanner and going through it a slice at a time, the way you might read a shut book by its shadow. Miriam’s job had been to tell the software which shadows were letters. She did it for four months. What came out at the end was not a prayer or a dedication or a king’s name. It was instructions. Someone had written, in small careful Greek, how to work the thing.

She had gone outside and stood in the car park of the institute in Athens for a long time.

It was the first thing she had ever read that no living person had read before her. She was twenty-six. Fourteen months earlier she had walked out of the place where she had expected to spend her life, and she had believed, until that afternoon, that she was finished with being addressed across a great distance by people who expected to be understood.

It was the defining thing. Not for what they found, but for what it taught her. Patience. The gap between what you think you are looking for and what is actually there. And the particular silence that falls over a team when something comes up out of the water that changes what you thought you knew. She had never forgotten that silence. She had been listening for it, in one room and another, for twenty years.

She had not heard from Locke since. He had not written to congratulate her. He had not answered when she wrote to thank him.

Until three weeks ago, when his name appeared at the foot of a request for a cultural analyst on a geological survey in the North Atlantic. Routine. Unglamorous. A month of telegraph cable.

She had asked the agency whether he had said why. He had not. She had asked whether he was aboard. He was not. He was eighty-one and, as far as anyone could tell her, had not left Durham in a decade.

She opened a message to him now. She got as far as *Dear Professor Locke, I am writing from* and stopped, because she found she did not know what the question was. *Why did you send me?* was absurd. He had sent her to look at rubbish on the sea floor, and she had looked at it. Nothing had happened. A geologist had a date she could not explain, and there was a bright mark on a sonar image with no crack above it.

She deleted the message.

Then she lay down, in her boots, and looked at the underside of the upper bunk, and did not sleep.

### 04:40.

The prep room smelled of neoprene and yesterday’s coffee, which was not kind.

Natasha was already there, with the dive plan on a tablet and her hair tied back, looking as though she had slept eight hours and eaten a proper breakfast. She had probably done both. She took one look at Miriam and said nothing, and turned her head a little to the left, the way she did, to bring her good ear round.

“I went to the lab,” Miriam said.

“I saw the light.”

“Iyer ran it again. Fresh chips.”

“And?”

“Two hundred and three. Plus or minus nine.”

Natasha wrote it down. She did not say *hm* or raise an eyebrow or offer a view. She wrote the number and the error and the time, in the margin of the dive plan, in the small square hand that stayed legible in any sea.

“What do you make of it?” Miriam asked.

“I think I’ll look at it,” Natasha said. “In about two hours.”

That was the whole of her position, and Miriam had come to find it restful. In a month she had not once heard Natasha say what a thing meant. She said what the instrument had said, and stopped. At first Miriam had taken it for caution. Lately she had begun to think it was closer to a vow.

“Then look at this as well.”

She took the notebook out and opened it to the page and held it across. It did not occur to her not to. It had been a guess for four days. As of an hour ago it was a guess with something to check.

Natasha read the two lines. Then she pulled up the midnight profile on her own tablet, without being asked, and went over it from the top down, exactly as Miriam had.

“No fissure above it,” she said.

“Not one I can see.”

“It could be under the resolution. A hand’s width wouldn’t show.”

“That’s what I think.”

“So we look for a crack.”

“We look for a crack. And when we find it, I write four lines about somebody’s lost transponder, and Kamala keeps her date, and Alistair is unbearable for a week.”

“He’s unbearable now.”

“A different week.”

Natasha almost smiled. She gave the notebook back, and added a line to the dive plan: *Survey flow surface above object for fissures before anything else. MC.* She had put Miriam’s initials on it. She always put down whose idea a thing was.

Then she went through the sequence. Miriam had heard it four times before and knew its shape, and her hands had already begun checking her suit while Natasha talked. Hatch closed at six. Seventy minutes down. Four hours on the bottom, if the battery agreed. Seventy up. Hess to hold station over the site while they surveyed the top of the flow. Samples from the contact, if they could reach it, for Iyer. Cameras running throughout.

Miriam heard about half of it. Somewhere around the battery she found that she was looking at the telemetry monitor over the bench and thinking about nothing at all. Something about time. Two hundred thousand years of it, and a clean flat line of squares.

“I lost you,” Natasha said.

“You did. I’m sorry. Say the last part again?”

Natasha said it again, in the same words and the same order, without the least sign of impatience.

“Am I this foggy because of the patch,” Miriam said, “or is the swell doing something to my inner ear? I honestly can’t tell whether I need a doctor or just coffee.”

“You need to have slept,” said Natasha. “You can’t have either of the others.”

There were worse ways to spend a month. Miriam thought it as she zipped the notebook into the chest pocket of her suit, where it sat against her breastbone like a second, smaller heart. Competent people, a problem that would not behave, and an ocean to look at. She had come aboard expecting to be bored, and had been, and had found that she liked it. Tomorrow it would be over. There would be a crack.

### 05:40.

The aft deck was floodlit and wet and moving. The swell had come up in the night. It was not enough to stop a launch, but it was enough that you chose your moment to cross open deck and kept a hand on something while you did.

The Wren sat on her cradle under the A-frame with her hatch open, and going round her with a torch was the deck technician, Tobias, who was twenty-four and the only person on the ship who was reliably cheerful before sunrise. He was singing. He usually was. It carried over the generators, something with a good deal of hallelujah in it, and in four weeks nobody had had the heart to ask him to stop. As he went he laid his free hand flat on each hatch seal in turn, the way you would steady a horse.

“Morning, Dr. Calder! Morning, Dr. Varga! She’s ready for you. I’ve been all round her twice, and I’ve prayed over every seal.”

“Did you check them as well?” Miriam said.

“Checked them first. The praying’s extra.” He grinned. “No charge.”

She found that she was smiling. It was difficult not to, with Tobias. Twenty years ago she could have argued him into the ground in four languages, and she had no wish to. He said *God* the way other people said *the weather*. It was not a claim he was making. It was the air where he came from, and it seemed to agree with him.

“Is it true about the number?” he said, lower. “Everyone’s saying. Two hundred thousand years.”

So much for holding it out of the daily report.

“It’s a provisional date on a piece of rock,” Miriam said.

“That’s what Dr. Iyer said. She said it exactly like that.” He shook his head, delighted. “My dad says the world’s six thousand years old. I told him, Dad, I’ve *seen* the cores. He says the devil put them there. I said the devil’s got a lot of time on his hands, then.” He laughed. “Doesn’t trouble me. He made it as old as He made it. I only think it’s wonderful that there’s something down there nobody’s ever looked at, and you’re going to look at it, and I get to shut the door on you.”

“It will be a transponder, Tobias.”

“Then it’s a wonderful transponder,” he said, and went back to his seals.

Hess was at the foot of the ladder. He was the pilot: fifty-four, broad, with large quiet hands and a way of looking at people that she had at first found disconcerting and now found she relied on. He looked at Natasha. He looked at Miriam, for somewhat longer.

He considered what he saw as he might have considered a reading that was inside limits but only just. Then he tipped his head toward the ladder and stood aside.

Iyer had come out to see them off. She did it for every dive and pretended each time that she happened to be passing. She had a coat on over the clothes she had been wearing at half past two, and her hands were in her sleeves.

“Bring me a piece from right beside it,” she said to Natasha. “From the contact, if you can. Not a meter off. Beside it.”

“If Hess can get the arm in.”

“Hess can get the arm anywhere.” She turned to Miriam, and seemed about to say something, and did not. She only nodded, once, which Miriam understood to mean the conversation in the dry lab had taken place and need not be referred to.

Above them, on the boat deck, a man was standing at the rail without a coat. He was not doing anything. He was watching the launch the way you would watch a transaction. When Miriam looked up he did not look away, and after a moment she did.

One porthole was lit on the starboard side, low down, where the petrology bench was.

She climbed the ladder. The hatch was a steel ring just wide enough for her shoulders, and below it the sphere was already warm with instruments and smelled of rubber and old breath. She let herself down into it and found her seat by feel. Natasha came after her, neat and quick. Hess came last and filled what space there was.

Tobias’s face appeared in the ring of sky above them, upside down.

“God go with you,” he said, as he said to every dive, and swung the hatch over.

It came down with a sound like a bank vault. The singing, the generators, the wind and the sea all stopped at once, as though someone had lifted a needle from a record. In the quiet she could hear the dogs going home, one at a time, and then the flat of his hand, twice, on the steel over her head.

Hess ran his eye along the board.

“Ready?”

Natasha looked at Miriam.

“Ready when you are,” Miriam said.

It was time to go down.
