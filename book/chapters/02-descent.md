The submersible was called the Wren. Miriam had always thought that was a strange name for something designed to sink, but someone on the crew had explained, weeks ago, that the original Wren had been a cathedral builder, and that the vessel was named for the architect, not the bird. She had liked that and then immediately forgotten who told her.

There were three of them in the cabin. Miriam, Natasha, and the pilot — a man named Hess whose patience she had come to respect over the course of four dives. Hess did not narrate. He did not point things out. He operated the Wren with the unhurried precision of someone who had done this enough times that the vehicle had become an extension of his body, and he left the two scientists to do their jobs without making himself part of their conversation. She appreciated this more than she had ever told him.

This was her fifth. She knew the order of things by now. The crane took them off the deck and the sea took them off the crane, and for two or three minutes the sphere wallowed on the surface like a bottle while a man in a wetsuit walked about on the hull over their heads, casting off. That was the only part she minded. The patch behind her ear was supposed to see to it and mostly did. Then Hess opened the vents, and the water came up over the ports in a line, green and full of bubbles, and at about twenty meters the motion stopped. It did not ease. It stopped. The swell was a thing that happened to the top of the ocean, and they were no longer at the top.

“Vents shut,” Hess said, to the recorder and not to them. “Leaving surface. Oh six oh nine.”

Natasha wrote it down. She wrote everything down. The dive plan was clipped to her knee, four pages, and Miriam could see the line that had been added at the top in the small hours in Natasha’s square hand. *Survey flow surface above object for fissures before anything else. MC.* It looked official there. It looked like something a person had thought about for longer than forty minutes at three in the morning.

The descent took time. It always took time. That was the thing about deep water that surprised people who had never been — not the pressure or the cold or the dark, but the duration. You sit in a metal shell and you sink, and the sinking takes longer than you think it should, and the light changes so gradually that you don’t notice the moment it leaves. First blue. Then a deeper blue that isn’t really blue anymore, more like the memory of blue. Then nothing. The monitors glow. The instruments hum. Your own breathing becomes the loudest thing in the world.

Nothing drove them. That had surprised her too, the first time. The Wren went down the way a stone goes down, on weight alone, a stack of iron plates clipped under her belly that Hess would drop when it was time to come home. Half a meter a second. A slow walk. Two thousand meters at a slow walk is seventy minutes, and there is no way to hurry it and nothing to do in it, and the people who are good at this work are the ones who can be left alone with seventy minutes.

Miriam watched the depth gauge and thought about nothing in particular. This was one of her skills, though she wouldn’t have called it that — the ability to let her mind go slack during the parts of an operation that didn’t require her. Natasha was reviewing the bot’s overnight telemetry on her tablet, annotating, organizing. Hess was watching his instruments. Miriam was waiting. She was not impatient. She was only present, in the way an animal is present, aware of her environment without being engaged by it.

The gauge did the arithmetic for her whether she wanted it or not. Every ten meters was another atmosphere. At a hundred meters the sea was pressing on the sphere eleven times as hard as the air had on deck. At five hundred, fifty-one. The hull was titanium as thick as her wrist and it did not care, but it did answer. Somewhere past three hundred meters the seat frames began to tick, very quietly, as the sphere drew in around them by a millimeter or two. Hess had told her about that on her first dive, in six words, before it happened. It was the only thing he had ever warned her of.

It got cold. The heat went out through the metal into water that was four degrees and would be four degrees all the way down. By the half hour the inside of the sphere was sweating, and she had her hands up her sleeves.

Between three hundred meters and eight hundred the dark outside was not empty. Hess kept the floods off on the way down, to save the battery, and in the black beyond the ports there were sparks. Blue ones, mostly. A thread of them, a slow burst, a single point that drifted up past the glass and went out. Things that made their own light, signaling to each other, or to nothing. She had asked Natasha about it on the second dive and had got twenty minutes on luciferin. She did not ask again. She watched them the way you watch the lights of a town from a train, and after eight hundred meters there were fewer, and then there were none.

There was a net bag lashed to the basket outside, under the forward camera. She could see it on the monitor when Hess cycled the views. Tobias had tied it there. It was full of foam cups, the ordinary white kind from the galley, and every one had a name on it in marker pen. It was a thing ships did. You sent a cup to the bottom and the sea squeezed the air out of the foam, and it came back the size of a thimble, hard as a nut, with your name on it very small. The cook had one. The bosun had one for a grandchild. Tobias had drawn a cross on his. Nobody had asked her whether she wanted a cup, and she had been faintly relieved, and then faintly put out.

“You didn’t sleep,” Natasha said. She did not look up from the tablet. She was sitting, as she always sat, with her good ear toward the middle of the sphere.

“An hour. After I left you.”

“I didn’t either.” She made a mark, and scrolled. “I kept flying the lanes. In my head. I kept finding it on lane three.”

“Finding what?”

“Your crack. A nice big one. Half a meter across, with a cable going down it.” Natasha considered the screen. “I was very pleased. I had the paragraph written. Kamala keeps her date, Alistair has to buy the wine, and I go back to measuring manganese like a sane person.”

“You want it to be a crack.”

“Of course I want it to be a crack. Don’t you?”

Miriam thought about that honestly, because Natasha would know if she did not.

“I want to know which it is,” she said. “A crack would be knowing.”

“That isn’t the same as wanting it.”

“No. It’s what I’ve got.”

Natasha nodded, as if that were a reading off an instrument and a reasonable one. They did not say any more about it. It was one of the things Miriam had come to like about her. She did not need a conversation to arrive anywhere. She would say what she had, and take what you had, and go back to her numbers, and the thing would simply be known between you from then on, like a depth.

At eleven hundred meters Natasha said something about the thermal gradient being steeper than predicted. Miriam filed this without responding. At sixteen hundred meters the external cameras showed nothing but particulate drifting upward in the lights like slow snow. At two thousand meters Hess made a small adjustment and the Wren tilted almost imperceptibly, angling toward the survey coordinates.

“Twenty minutes,” he said. That was more than he usually offered.

He let the first of the weights go. She felt it more than heard it, a knock through the frame and a lightness after, as two iron plates fell away into the dark beneath them. The gauge slowed, and crept, and stopped at one thousand nine hundred and ninety-eight. Now the Wren weighed exactly what the water weighed. She hung where she was put. Hess touched the thrusters and they began to move across the bottom instead of toward it, and that was always the moment the dive became a place.

It was not much of a place. A grey slope going by under the floods. Pillows of old lava, heaped and split, like a cellar full of sacks. Once a fish, long and pale with a head too big for it, that hung a hand above the rock and did not trouble to get out of the light. Miriam had expected, on her first dive, that the deep would feel hostile. It did not. It felt like a room that nobody had ever come into, and that had no opinion about being entered now.

The site came into view on the monitors before it came into view through the porthole, which was how it always worked — you saw the sonar rendering first, the false-color topology, the shape of the seafloor assembled from data rather than light. Miriam had spent enough time looking at these renderings that she could read them the way she read a script. Structure. Pattern. The grammar of geology.

The basalt field looked unremarkable on approach. Fractured, angular, the kind of volcanic terrain you found all along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. Dark rock split by cooling and pressure into geometries that were complex but explicable. She’d seen this a hundred times.

They had known for nine days that there was something in it. The bot had found that much, flying its patient lines fifty meters up: a hard return under the surface of one flow, a bright smudge in a place where the rock ought to have been the same all the way through. A smudge was all it was at that range. It was enough to hold a ship on station and start an argument about a date. It was not enough to say what the thing was, or how it had got there, and on the second of those questions Miriam had an opinion, and the opinion was at the top of the dive plan with her initials on it.

“Fissures first,” Natasha said.

“Fissures first.”

Hess brought the floods up.

It was the first time she had seen the flow by light, and it was not what the false color had led her to expect. It was a pavement. The lava had come out in a sheet and lain flat and cooled from the top, and as it cooled it had shrunk and cracked, the way mud cracks in a dry pond, into plates a few meters across. Five sides, mostly. Some six. They fitted against one another like the scales on a tortoise, and the joints between them ran away into the dark in every direction, black on black, each plate with a skin of glass on it that threw the light back in dull patches.

It was very clean. She noticed that before she knew why it mattered. There was sediment, but not much of it, and what there was lay in the low places, a grey fluff in the angles where three plates met, like snow that has blown into ditches and left the road bare.

“There’s a current along the crest,” Natasha said. She had seen her looking. “A few centimeters a second, all the time. It keeps the high ground swept. Come a hundred meters downslope and you’d be up to your knees.”

“And the joints?”

“That’s what we’re here to see.”

The plan was a simple one, and tedious, which is what plans ought to be. A square thirty meters on a side, centered on the bot’s smudge. Hess would fly it in lanes two meters apart at an altitude of two meters, down one lane and back up the next, as a man mows a lawn. The forward sonar would look into the rock. The cameras would look at the top of it. And every time a lane crossed a joint, the three of them would look at the joint.

What they were looking for was a way in. That was all her opinion came to. A thing that turns up inside two-hundred-thousand-year-old rock is either two hundred thousand years old, or it arrived later through a gap. Basalt cracks as it cools. Cracks open, and stay open, and things fall down them. This ridge had been worked for sixty years by every nation that could float a hull. The sea floor was not virgin. There were cables on it, and anchors, and instrument frames, and the litter of ten thousand deployments, and any one of those could put a cylinder a meter long into a hole. So before anyone said a word about what the object was, they would find the hole. It was the dull explanation. In her experience the dull explanation was usually standing right behind you with its coat on.

“Lane one,” Hess said.

Natasha started her clock.

A lane took four minutes. The plates went by under the lights at the pace of a slow walk, and at each joint Hess slowed further, and Natasha read off a number from the sonar, and Miriam looked.

The joints were tight. That was the first thing, and it went on being the first thing for an hour. They were hairlines. Where the camera could see down into one, it saw down a finger’s length and then the two faces met, glass against glass, with nothing between that you could have slid a knife into. Natasha had a scale laser on them, two red dots ten centimeters apart, and against the dots the joints were nothing. A millimeter. Two.

“Tight,” Natasha said, and wrote it down.

“Tight.”

Lane two. At the end of each one Hess pivoted the Wren on her own length with a touch of the thrusters, and the lights swung over the pavement, and the next lane began. It was like reading a very long document for one word. Miriam had done a great deal of that in her life. She knew how the mind goes soft in the fortieth page and starts to supply the word where it isn’t, and she knew the cure for it, which is to stop expecting anything. She let the plates go by. Tight. Tight. A junction of three, with its little drift of grey. Tight.

She had learned the cure on a barge off Antikythera, at twenty-six, as the most junior person aboard. They had given her the job nobody else wanted, which was to sit at a scanner for ten hours a day and go over a plate of green bronze the size of her palm, looking for letters a millimeter and a half high in a surface that was mostly disease. On the third day she had found a whole word. She had been so pleased that she called the director over, and he had looked at it for some time and told her, not unkindly, that it was a crack and two pits. After that she stopped looking for letters. She looked at what was there. The letters, when they came, came by themselves.

On lane four the word was there.

She saw it before Natasha did. A joint ahead of them, running square across the lane, and it was not a hairline. It was a dark band as wide as two fingers, sharp-edged, dead straight, and black all the way down.

“Hess.”

He had already slowed. He brought the Wren over it and stopped, and tipped the forward camera down, and for a moment the three of them looked into it and Miriam felt the whole night’s argument come to rest. There it was. She had said there would be a way in, and there was a way in, and the world was the size she had thought it was.

Natasha put the laser on it. The two red dots fell across the band. One of them landed on the near plate, and the other landed on the far plate, and the far dot was lower.

“It’s a step,” Natasha said.

It was a step. The far plate sat three centimeters below the near one, that was all, the way one paving stone will settle beside another, and the lights coming in low from the vehicle had laid the shadow of the edge across the joint like a stripe of paint. Hess lifted the Wren half a meter and the band thinned. He lifted her another half and it was gone. Under it the joint was a hairline, glass to glass.

“Tight,” Natasha said, and wrote it down. She did not say anything else. She did not need to.

Miriam let her breath go. A crack and two pits. She had been off that barge for twenty years, and it was still the most useful thing anyone had ever told her.

On lane five the sonar picture changed.

It was nothing to look at through the port. The pavement was the same pavement. But on the monitor, where the forward sonar drew the inside of the rock in false color, there was a shadow under the plates ahead of them, and then, as they came over it, a shape.

Nobody said anything. Natasha marked the time.

“We’re over it,” Hess said.

“Keep the lane,” Miriam said. “We’ll come back to it. Joints first.”

It cost her something to say that. She did not look at the monitor again until the lane was done, and she noticed Natasha not looking at it either, with some effort, and she thought that they were probably both being absurd. But a search is only a search if you finish it. If you stop to stare at the thing you hoped to find, you have stopped looking for the thing that would explain it away, and she had not come two thousand meters down to be the sort of person who did that.

So they flew lane five to its end over the shape in the rock, and looked at the joints above it.

There were four. They ran round one plate, a four-sided plate, a little larger than its neighbors. Hess took the Wren along each of them in turn at the speed of a hand moving across a page, with the camera nearly on the glass.

They were tight. The northern one was a hairline. The southern one was a hairline. The western one was so fine that Natasha lost it twice against the texture of the rind and had to ask Hess to go back. The eastern one ran off under a fold of the flow at the plate’s edge, where the lava had rucked up like a carpet, and they could not see into it from above at all. Natasha wrote *E joint: obscured from above. Check from side* on the plan, and drew a box round it.

“No fissure above it,” Miriam said.

It was what she had said at ten past three that morning over a sonar print, and Natasha had heard her say it then. It sounded different down here.

“Not from above,” Natasha said. She was careful. “Three of four are closed. I can’t speak to the fourth.”

“Write that.”

“I have.”

Lane six. Lane seven. There were nine more lanes in the square and they flew them all, because that was the plan, and because a hole ten meters off to one side with a passage running under the plates was not impossible and would have done just as well. The plates went by. The joints were tight. Hess said nothing whatever, and the Wren turned on her length at the end of each lane with the same small sound.

It was on lane eleven, well south of the shape and nowhere near it, that Natasha said, “Hard return. Port side. That’s not rock.”

Hess stopped the vehicle and swung the lights.

It lay at a junction where three plates met, in one of the little grey drifts, and it was the most ordinary object Miriam had ever been pleased to see. A cylinder. Forty centimeters long, as thick as a man’s thigh, with a steel bail at one end and a frayed tail of rope still shackled to the bail. It was orange under a crust of brown. There was a maker’s plate on the side of it, and the camera could read the first three letters and a serial number.

“Transponder,” Hess said.

“Acoustic beacon,” Natasha said. “Somebody’s mooring let go. Twenty years ago. Thirty.” She was already writing. “It isn’t ours. That’s an old housing. Nobody’s built them in that shape since before I was at university.”

Miriam looked at it for a while.

It was sitting on top of the pavement. That was the thing about it. It had come down from the surface on its own weight, through two kilometers of water, and landed on a field of basalt with ten thousand cracks in it, and it had not gone down a crack. It had not gone down one because there was not a crack on that whole flow you could have got a pencil into. It lay in the open in a handful of silt with its rope beside it, exactly where it had fallen, and it would lie there until the sun went out.

“There’s my transponder,” she said.

Natasha looked up. For a moment she did not follow, and then she did, and her mouth went straight in the way it did when she was not going to laugh.

“You said you’d write four lines about it.”

“I did. You write faster.”

“I’m writing six. I want the serial.” She finished, and capped her pen. “It’s on the rock, Miriam. It isn’t in it.”

“I know.”

“So it’s no use to you.”

“It’s a great deal of use to me. It’s what the answer would have looked like.” She watched the orange thing go out of the lights as Hess took up the lane again. “That’s how a lost object lies on this flow. On top, in the dirt, with a rope on it. I’d like that in the record next to the other one.”

“It’s in.”

It will be a transponder, she had told Tobias on deck, not three hours ago, and he had said that then it was a wonderful transponder. He had been right about that. It was a wonderful transponder. It had answered her question perfectly, and the answer was no.

They finished the square. Lane sixteen ended at 08:41. Natasha totted up her column and read it aloud for the recorder: sixteen lanes, two hundred and eleven joint crossings, no joint wider than three millimeters, no fissure, no collapse, no way in from above within fifteen meters of the object. One joint obscured. One piece of debris, lying on the surface, unconnected.

Miriam took out her notebook. Under *No way in. Look for the cut* she wrote the time, and *None from above. E joint not seen.* Then she put it back in her chest pocket, and it sat against her sternum where it had sat all night.

“All right,” she said. “Now show me.”

Hess took them back over the four-sided plate and brought the Wren down until the skids were a meter off the glass. He switched the sonar from its search setting to the fine one, the narrow beam, the one that had to be nearly touching a thing to see it at all.

Then the rendering updated as they closed distance and the resolution sharpened, and she saw the site.

She didn’t say anything. Neither did Natasha, though Natasha leaned forward slightly, which for Natasha was equivalent to someone else shouting.

The cavity was visible on the sonar as a void in the basalt. A pocket of emptiness inside solid rock. That by itself was not unusual — basalt forms cavities all the time as gas escapes during cooling. But basalt cavities are irregular. They’re bulbous, asymmetric, shaped by the physics of gas expansion in cooling magma. They look organic in the way that all natural formations look organic — imprecise, opportunistic, governed by local conditions rather than design.

This cavity was none of those things.

On the sonar rendering it appeared as a clean geometric form. Not a sphere. Not a cube. Something more particular — an elongated ovoid with uniform wall thickness and a smooth internal surface that the sonar was reading as nearly frictionless. The walls of the cavity were equidistant from the object at its center at every point. As though the rock had been asked, politely, to make room.

Miriam looked at it and felt something she did not have a word for. It was not excitement and it was not dread. It was something between recognition and vertigo. The feeling you get when a pattern you’ve been sensing at the edge of your awareness suddenly becomes visible, and it’s bigger than you thought, and it doesn’t fit any category you have.

“That’s not a gas pocket,” Natasha said.

“No.”

“The symmetry — ”

“I know.”

Hess brought them closer. The external cameras began to pick up the site in actual light now — the Wren’s floods illuminating the basalt, the dark volcanic rock resolving into texture and detail. The fracture patterns in the surrounding field were normal. The basalt was basalt. It did what basalt does. And then, in the middle of it, there was a place where it had done something else entirely.

The surface above the cavity was slightly different from the surrounding rock. It was not discolored, exactly. It was more that the grain was finer. As though the basalt in that area had cooled under different conditions — more slowly, more evenly, with more control than the chaotic thermal environment of a volcanic seafloor should allow. A geologist would need to examine samples to confirm this. Miriam didn’t need samples. She could see it. The rock above the cavity had the quality that materials develop when they are worked with intention. It had not been carved, and it had not been machined. It was something for which no one on this vessel, herself included, had vocabulary.

She had flown over that plate forty minutes ago and looked at nothing but its edges.

“Can you hold position here?” Natasha asked Hess.

“Already am.”

Natasha was pulling up the bot’s earlier scans, comparing them to what they were seeing now. Cross-referencing the overnight data with the live feed. Doing what good scientists do when confronted with the inexplicable — documenting, measuring, refusing to interpret until the data forced her to.

Miriam was not doing any of those things. Miriam was looking at the sonar rendering of the object inside the cavity. It was cylindrical. Roughly a meter long. Dense. The sonar return suggested a material that was neither rock nor metal but something with acoustic properties she hadn’t encountered before. It was just sitting there, inside its impossible chamber, in the middle of a basalt field at the bottom of the North Atlantic, two thousand meters below the surface, sealed in rock that was at minimum two hundred thousand years old.

Sealed. She had spent an hour establishing that word. Three joints closed and one not seen. No way in from above. A beacon thirty years old lying on the roof to show her what a late arrival looked like. She had come down with a dull explanation and a method for finding it, and she had applied the method honestly, and the dull explanation was not there.

Her notebook was in the chest pocket of her suit. She could feel its weight against her sternum. The half-formed question she’d written days ago — the one about the contact layer, the depositional context, the date that didn’t sit right — was in that notebook, and the question had just gotten much larger.

She didn’t reach for it. She just looked. For a long time she just looked.

Then she said to Natasha, very quietly, in a tone that she recognized even as she used it as the tone she had heard fall over the Antikythera recovery team the day the back plate came up from the water — the particular silence, the careful voice, the awareness that whatever you say next is going to be part of a record that people study long after you’re dead:

“We need to call the surface.”
