### North Atlantic, 2075. 05:10.

The forecast was on the long screen in the dry lab when Miriam came in, and nobody was looking at anything else.

It was a simple picture. A low was passing to the north, and it was sending a swell down ahead of it. Under the map ran a single line, wave height against the clock. It stood at a meter now. It crossed two and a half at 13:00, reached three by midnight, and was back under two by the next dawn.

“Two and a half is my limit,” Hess said. He was standing, as he always stood, with his back against something. “Past that I can’t hook her on. She stays out and we tow her like a dead whale, with three people inside. So she’s on deck by one. Seventy minutes up. I leave the bottom at eleven-thirty. Launch at six-twenty, I’m down at seven-thirty, and I have four hours.”

“Is four hours enough?” Iyer said.

“Four holes, four anchors, hook up, stand off, lift, come back, pick up. If nothing goes wrong, it’s three and a half.”

“And if something does?”

“Then it isn’t.”

Dunmore had the plate up on the second screen, stitched from four hundred frames. The flow had cracked as it cooled, the way mud does, into flat polygons a few meters across. Most had five sides. The one over the object had four. He had his clipboard against his chest.

“Four meters by two and a half, thirty centimeters thick. Three cubic meters. Call the density two point nine. Eight point seven tonnes on a dock. It pushes aside three point one of seawater, so on the wire it’s five point six.” He turned the clipboard round. He had done it by hand, and underlined it. “Your line is rated for twelve. Add a tonne for friction and bad luck.”

“Or wait for the swell to pass,” Natasha said. “It’s one day.”

“Tell them about the contact,” Iyer said.

Carrow had been up since three. It did not show, except that he had not shaved. “There’s a vessel nine miles northwest. She came up at ten past three and stopped. She doesn’t answer on any channel. I put a hydrophone over at four. Twin screws, and a hull mapper at twelve kilohertz on a slow sweep.” He let that sit. “It’s the set Dr. Calder heard from the bottom two days ago. I’ve compared the pulse.”

“Whose is she?” Dunmore said.

“I don’t know.”

“You don’t know, or you won’t say?”

“Would you be able to tell the difference, Alistair?”

He would not, and neither would anyone else in the room. Miriam thought it possible that Carrow was telling the plain truth. She thought he had arranged his life so that the plain truth and the other thing looked alike.

“They’ve had a mapper over this site twice,” Carrow said. “They don’t need to know what’s under that plate. They only need to know we stopped here for a week. A hull that size can carry a vehicle that doesn’t care about swell. So it comes up today, or we should assume it comes up without us.”

“That’s my decision,” Iyer said.

“It is. I’m telling you what I’d weigh.”

“I’ve weighed it.” She was fifty-eight, and Miriam had never seen her hurry a decision or put one off. “We go at six-twenty. Hess, you have the bottom until eleven-thirty and not one minute past it. Natasha, the box. Dr. Calder, the third seat.”

“It’s a rock job,” Miriam said. “Alistair should have it.”

Dunmore looked at her with open suspicion. “Why?”

“Because if that plate’s keyed on a side we can’t see, I won’t know it and you will.”

“I’ll know it from the van, where there is a desk and a tension gauge. Somebody up here should be able to count.” He lowered the clipboard. “It’ll come off in one piece. It’s the best-jointed plate I’ve seen in thirty years, and that’s what I don’t like about it. Joints go where the stress goes. They don’t go round things. And don’t ask me why what’s under it isn’t a cinder. I have a list. That’s at the top.”

“I wasn’t going to ask.”

“No. You’d already written it down.” He turned back to his screen. “You found the damned thing. Go and fetch it.”

“There’s a second matter,” Carrow said.

He had a case with him. Miriam had taken it for the grey one he had carried the dive records off in the night before. It was longer. He laid it on the bench and opened it, and it was a transit case, a meter and a half of it, with a cold pack down each side and a channel of foam in the middle the width of a man’s forearm.

“The sponsors’ vessel is fourteen hours out. She’ll put a boat alongside at first light tomorrow, when the swell’s gone down. Between the sea and that boat, the object is in this. It goes from the basket to the case. My tag goes on. Nobody opens it.”

“On whose authority?” Natasha said.

“Clause fourteen,” Iyer said, before he could. Her voice was quite level. “Recovered material of exceptional character passes to the sponsor’s custody. I signed it. I thought I was signing it for manganese nodules.”

“I’d like to tell you why,” Carrow said. “I don’t have to. I’d like to.”

“Go on.”

“From the moment it leaves the water, every hour is an hour that somebody will later say we did something to it. Warmed it. Scratched it. Swapped it. Made it.” He looked at each of them in turn. “You are five honest people, and you’ll be called liars by Thursday week. I can’t prevent that. I can make the story short. Out of the sea, into the case, tag on. The next hands on it are in a clean room, on camera, with strangers watching. That protects it. It also protects you.”

It was a good argument. Miriam turned it over and could not find the hole in it. That was what she disliked.

Iyer had found one. She took her glasses down out of her hair and put them on, which she did only to read.

“Passes to the sponsor’s custody *at the rail*,” she said. “That is the wording. I remember it because I asked what rail, and your lawyer said, the rail of the receiving vessel or her boat. Your boat will be at my rail at first light, Mr. Carrow. Until it is, that thing is recovered material aboard my ship, and I am the chief scientist.”

Carrow said nothing for a moment.

“That’s one night,” he said.

“It is one night. Your story will still be short.”

He did not argue with a fact. Miriam had seen that in him the night before, over the records. He stood and ran it, and then he nodded.

“My conditions. It stays in water, cold. Nothing is cut from it and nothing is taken out of it. My camera is on it every minute, and the originals are mine. And at first light it goes in this case, in whatever state it’s in.”

“Agreed.”

“I’d like to look at it,” Miriam said. She said it to Carrow, since it was his camera.

“I know you would, Dr. Calder.”

“She’ll look,” Iyer said, and closed the case herself.

### 06:20.

The launch was wet. The stern rose and fell a slow meter, and the Wren swung on her line like a bell before the sea took her weight. Tobias had gone round the hatch seal with his bare thumb, the whole circle, his lips moving. Then he shut it over their heads. The last Miriam saw of the day was his palm flat against the port, and grey water sliding up behind it.

Hess flooded the tanks. The motion stopped. It always stopped in the first twenty meters, as if a hand had been laid on the hull.

“Four hours,” Hess said, to nobody, and started his watch.

### 07:31.

There was no weather on the bottom. That was the hard thing to keep hold of.

The plate lay in the lights exactly as it had lain the day before, among its five-sided neighbors, and the water over it was as still as the air in a cellar. The swell that was coming for them was two kilometers overhead. Down here it existed only as Dunmore’s voice on the link, a breath and a third late, reading out a number that went up.

Miriam had no task in the sphere. So she made one. She kept the clock.

“Hole one, twenty-two minutes,” she said. “On time.”

Hess grunted. He was bent to the arm controls with his chin nearly on his chest. The bit was through the glass rind and into fresh rock, and the sound of it came up through the skids into their feet.

“Hole two, twenty-six. You’re four over.”

“Harder corner.”

“I’m not complaining. I’m counting.”

“Count quieter.”

Natasha, on Miriam’s left with her good ear turned inboard, very nearly smiled. It was cold in the sphere, as it always was by the second hour. The hull sweated. The three of them breathed each other’s air and did not speak of the thing a meter and a half under the plate. Miriam found that she was grateful for the drill.

Hole three, twenty-three minutes. Hole four, twenty-four. Then the anchors, steel sleeves that spread when Hess drove the pin, and a pull test on each. The yellow spreader bar came down out of the dark on the main line with its pinger ticking, and Hess walked the claw round four shackle pins while Miriam read him the time at every one. At 09:58 he said, “Four on.” He backed the Wren out north and upslope without being asked. At 10:03 he set her down on bare rock two hundred meters off and folded his arms.

“Nobody sits under a load,” he said. It was not addressed to anyone. He said it the way another man might have touched wood.

“Surface, Wren. Clear. Take your strain.”

### 10:05.

Nobody saw the lid come off.

That was Hess’s rule, and it was a good rule. It meant that the one event of the morning happened in the dark, with two hundred meters of black water between the plate and the nearest eye. What they had was Dunmore, and a number.

“Nought point three. That’s the bar and the chain.” A breath and a third. “Two tonnes. Four. Five. Five point six.” He had his lecturing voice on, and there was a rustle that was his clipboard. “It hasn’t stopped. It’s keyed. Six. Six point three.”

Miriam wrote each figure down. The recorder upstairs was writing them too, forty times a second, and her column was no use to anyone. She wrote them anyway.

“Five point seven. She’s hanging. I said five point six.”

“He’s pleased with himself,” Natasha said.

“He’s earned it.”

“He’s giving her three meters, and then the bridge walks us east. Seven point two.”

Hess unfolded his arms.

“That’s the ship coming up on a big one. The compensator’s paying out. It’s—” The voice stopped, and that silence was not the water’s. “It’s on its stops. It has no more travel. Nine point one.”

Miriam understood it as she would have understood a sentence. The machine that kept the wire still had run out of reach. Now the plate was tied to the ship, and the ship was going up and down two meters with five and a half tonnes of basalt on the end of two kilometers of rope.

“Two point nought. It’s gone slack. It’s falling. Kamala—”

They heard it then. It came through the water and the hull as one dull knock, very low, like a fist on the far side of a wall. Natasha had her fingers flat against the sphere. Hess did not move at all.

“Ten point four,” Dunmore said. His voice had gone thin and quick. “That was the snatch. The line’s rated twelve. He’s putting it down. He’s putting it down now, wherever it is. Three. One. Nought point three.” A long breath, chewed by the modem. “It’s down. Wren, it’s down, and I can’t tell you where. I won’t have that load on that line a second time and neither will Kamala. That was your lift. There isn’t another.”

“Understood,” Hess said. He was already driving.

### 10:19.

The plate came up out of the dark at an angle, and for a moment Miriam could not read what she was seeing.

It had gone back most of the way into its own socket. Its western edge was down where it had always been. But its eastern edge had landed on the lip of the next plate over and stayed there, a hand’s length short of home, and one corner of it stood on a block of rubble the size of a loaf, which had not existed a quarter of an hour ago. So the plate lay tilted, like the lid of a box closed on a pencil. Along the eastern side there was a black slot, half a meter high at the corner and tapering away to nothing.

The yellow bar lay across the top in a tangle of chain. The line went up from it into the dark, and it was moving. It tightened, and sagged, and tightened.

“Surface, Wren. Fire the release on the bar. I don’t want that line.”

A breath and a third. Another. Then the bar gave a small jerk, and the line slid up out of the lights and was gone.

Hess brought them round to the east side and set the skids down a meter back from the lip. He put the lights into the slot.

It was there. It lay one and a half meters in, pale against black glass. The rock under it had taken its shape, a long smooth trough, shining. Not a grain of anything was on it.

“No,” Hess said.

Nobody had asked him anything.

“That’s a roof with one leg. I don’t go under a load. I told you that the first day and I’m telling you now.”

“The arm reaches one point nine,” Natasha said.

“I know what the arm reaches.”

“Time,” Miriam said. “Ten twenty-four. You have sixty-six minutes.”

She sent Dunmore three frames. A picture went up the link in pieces, forty seconds a frame, and they sat and watched the bar crawl across the screen three times. The rubble block stood in the lights with a crack running clean through it from top to bottom.

When Dunmore came back he had stopped being pleased and had stopped being frightened. What was left was a man who knew rock.

“Listen. It’s on three points. Its own west edge, the neighbor’s lip, and that block. Take the block away and it’s on two, and it hinges down about the west edge like a trapdoor. It can’t slide east. The lip stops it. So you’re safe where you sit, and I’d stake my pension on it. Anything under it is under it.”

“How long does the block hold?” Miriam said.

“It’s cracked glass with two tonnes on it. It has held for fourteen minutes. That is the whole of the data.” There was a pause that was his own. “And that isn’t a void, under there. I said a void. It’s a mold. The flow came up to the thing and stopped a hand’s breadth off, all the way round, and chilled. I’ve seen lava do that round a wet tree. You get a pipe in the rock the shape of the trunk.”

“And the tree?”

“The tree doesn’t survive. It’s the same item on my list. I’ve underlined it. Get on.”

“The arm I’ll risk,” Hess said. He was not speaking to the surface. “If the plate comes down on the arm, I blow the arm off and we go home. That’s what the rule is for. It isn’t the arm.”

“Then what is it?”

He turned his head and looked at her. It was the longest speech she had yet had from him.

“Where it’s lying, it’s safe. It’s under the plate, in a hole its own shape. If that lid drops, it drops back where it came from and the thing never knows. It’s been safe there since before there were people. The only time it’s in the way is while I’m dragging it out through that slot.” He looked back at the screen. “So I’m not the one to say.”

The link hissed. Then it was Iyer, slow and quite clear.

“Dr. Calder. You can see it and I can’t.”

That was all. She had said it once before in other words, and she did not spend a sentence twice.

Miriam looked at the slot.

She wanted to see the thing in good light, on a bench, with a lens. She wanted that badly. She knew it, and so she did with it what she did with the lag on the link. She took it off before she judged what was left.

What was left was this. Leave it, and it was safe from the rock, and it belonged to whoever came next with a wire and a calm day. Carrow had said who that might be, and his arithmetic had been sound. Take it, and for eighty seconds a thing that had outlasted the ice ages would lie in the path of a trapdoor, because she had told a man to put it there.

The day before, she had weighed three people against it and taken two seconds. There were no people in this one. It was the one object, weighed against itself.

“How slowly can you bring it out?” she said.

“Two centimeters a second. Any slower and I’m just under there longer.”

“Don’t lift it. Slide it. Keep it down in its own trough until it’s past the lip. If the plate comes, it comes on the arm and not on that.”

Hess thought about it for the length of one breath.

“That’s right,” he said. “That’s how I’d do it.”

“Then go in.”

### 10:31.

The arm went into the slot a joint at a time.

The wrist camera showed them the underside of the plate going by a hand above the lens, black and rippled, and the walls of the trough, and their own light coming back off glass. Hess had the cradle open and was easing it along the floor with the tips of two fingers on the stick.

Something ticked.

It was a small sound, like a stove cooling. It came through the skids. Hess’s fingers stopped.

“Time,” he said.

“Ten forty-four.”

He waited. Miriam counted ten. The block stood in the corner of the main camera with its crack no wider.

He went on.

The cradle slid under the near end and along. The thing settled into it without a mark. Natasha had not taken her eyes from the wrist readout in ten minutes, and now she said, “Load cell. Four newtons.”

“Say again.”

“Four. It’s a meter long and it weighs a tin of soup.”

“Later,” Hess said.

Miriam wrote *4 N* and the time and nothing else. There would be an hour, somewhere, to be astonished in.

He drew it back. Two centimeters a second. A meter and six-tenths. She counted it aloud for him without being asked, in tens. Her voice was perfectly steady. Her free hand had closed on the notebook through her jacket until the wire binding hurt. Sixty. Seventy. The pale end came out from under the black edge into the open lights. Eighty.

“Clear of the lip,” Natasha said.

Nobody breathed out. Hess swung it over the basket and laid it down in the foam, and Natasha read the lid shut at 10:58, and the temperature, and the latch, twice.

Then he backed the Wren off the rock. He did it very gently, with the thrusters turned away. The plate stayed where it was.

It was still standing on its one leg when the lights left it. There were four holes in it with steel in them, and a yellow bar on top. Miriam watched it on the aft camera until it was a grey shape, and then a guess.

“It might stand a year,” Natasha said.

“It might have come down while I was under it,” Hess said. He was writing on his knee board. “You can have it either way. Nobody’s going to know.”

Miriam looked at the page in front of her. It had a column of times down it, and *4 N*, and that was all.

She had done the sum in the margin on the way out, because it was a sum she could do. A meter by twenty centimeters was thirty-one liters and a half. The thing pushed aside thirty-two kilos of ocean and weighed four newtons more than that. On a dock it would be a large dog. Where it had lain, it had been the weight of the water round it, to one part in a hundred. Bone was nearly twice that. So it was not solid.

That was the whole of her record. She had made no drawing. She had not measured how it lay, or which way its ends pointed, or what was under it. She had taken a thing out of the only setting it would ever have, in thirty-four minutes, and written down the hour. A man in Durham had once failed a student for less, and she had thought him right.

“Eleven-oh-six,” she said. “You have twenty-four minutes in hand.”

“I’ll take them,” Hess said, and dropped his weights.

### 12:16.

The ascent was seventy minutes of nothing, and then the sea took hold of them again.

It began at about thirty meters as a slow lean, one way and then the other. By ten it was a swing. When the Wren broke the surface she rolled until the port on Miriam’s side was green water and the one on Natasha’s was sky, and then she rolled back, and Natasha put her hand over her mouth and kept it there.

The box was outside, in the basket, in that. For seventy minutes two hundred atmospheres had been coming off it, ten meters at a time. Nobody had been able to look. Nobody could look now.

“He’s got one go at this,” Hess said. He meant the hook.

Miriam saw it through the upper port in pieces. The ship’s stern, enormous, lifting. A line coming down. Then a pair of boots landed on the hull over her head, and a body dropped flat along the Wren’s back with one arm hooked through the lifting bail. A sea went clean over him. When it had gone he was still there. His free hand came up with the hook in it, and missed, and waited through the next roll with what seemed to her a terrible patience, and did not miss.

“On,” Hess said. It was the first time she had heard him sound relieved.

### 12:44.

The plan for the deck was a canvas sling on two poles, and two men to carry it.

It was a sound plan. Natasha had measured the sling against the inside of the box the night before. What she had not been able to measure was how the thing would lie in the box when it arrived, and it had arrived pressed down into the foam along its whole length. There was no getting canvas under it without rolling it over.

“Don’t roll it,” Natasha said.

She was grey in the face and had her hair flat on one side from the headrest. She had the lid up on its spring. The drain at the foot of the box had been opened to trim the weight for the crane, and cold water was running out of it across the deck in a clear sheet.

Miriam looked over her shoulder.

It was pale, and wet, and whole. It lay in the white foam as it had lain in the black trough. There was no mark on it, no bloom, no crack. She had been afraid for seventy minutes of something she had not let herself picture. Now that it had not happened, she could not have said what it was.

“Intact,” Carrow said. He was at her elbow, wet to the knees, with his cuff toward the box.

“On this side,” Natasha said. “The water’s going. I want it in the tank.”

The tank stood by the wet lab door on a wheeled frame, a steel trough of seawater chilled to four degrees. It was eleven steps aft. The deck between was tilting slowly through ten degrees and back, with a film of water running on it. The two men with the sling looked at the box and at each other.

Tobias did not wait for them to decide.

He was still streaming from the hook-up. He had no gloves on. He had taken them off at dawn for the hatch seal and had not put them back. He went down on one knee beside the box and slid both forearms under the thing, at the third-points, the way a man gets his arms under a sleeping child.

“Adeyemi,” Carrow said, sharply.

He lifted it.

Nobody had told him to. Nobody stopped him either. By the time anyone might have, stopping him meant a young man standing still on a moving deck with thirty-two kilos of it across his arms. He stood. It came up out of the foam and shed water down the front of his coverall. He held it against his chest and turned aft.

Miriam counted, because she counted things. He took four steps, and stopped with his knees bent while the deck fell away from him. He waited for it. It came back. He took five more. He waited again, with his chin tucked down over the thing and his lips moving, and the water ran off his elbows. Two more. Eleven. The men with the sling walked beside him, one on each side, with their hands out and nothing in them.

At the tank he bent his knees and let it down through the surface. The water closed over it. He kept his arms under until it lay on the rack, and then he drew them out and stood back, soaked to the shoulder, breathing through his mouth.

He looked at his hands. Then he looked at Miriam. He chose her out of everyone on the fantail, and she understood why. He had heard her on the link for six days, flat and sure about every number. To Tobias, a person that sure was a person who believed.

“It’s warm,” he said.

Natasha already had the probe in the tank. She did not look up from the readout.

“It’s four degrees. Your hands are cold.”

“No, ma’am.” He was not arguing. He was smiling a little, as a man smiles who has been told the time by someone whose watch has stopped. “It’s warm.”

His hands were red to the wrist. He had been on deck since five in a wet wind, and in the sea to his neck at noon, and then he had put his arms into a box of meltwater. Miriam thought that Natasha was right. She thought it was hardly worth being right about.

She had written down everything else that day, to the tenth of a tonne. She did not write that down. It was not an observation. It was a deckhand with cold hands.

Hess climbed out last, as he always did. He looked at the empty box, and the tank, and Tobias’s sleeves.

“Who carried it?”

“I did.”

Hess looked at him a moment longer. Then he nodded once, and went to see to his weights.

They wheeled the tank inside. Iyer asked Tobias to shut the door, and he shut it, from the outside. Through the round glass Miriam saw him go to the rail and take his phone out of his chest pocket. He stood with his back to the wind, typing with two stiff thumbs. The crew had a welfare link for an hour each afternoon. She thought nothing of it, and turned to the tank.

### 15:05.

Natasha went down the object a hand at a time with the lens on its boom.

No cracks. No bubbles rising. No cloud in the water round it. She called each hand’s breadth clear, and Iyer ticked it, and Carrow stood at the foot of the tank with his cuff turned to it and did not shift his weight. At the end of the first pass Dunmore said, “Well, it didn’t explode,” and for Dunmore that was a toast.

Then Natasha stopped, eleven centimeters from the far end, and brought the lens back.

“Kamala.”

It was a line. It ran round the whole circumference as true as a lathe cut, and it was finer than a hair. Miriam had to be shown where to look.

“Then it was always there,” Carrow said.

“You have yesterday’s originals. I had the macro on that end for six minutes. Twenty microns a pixel. Look.”

He looked. He did it himself, on his own reader, with his back half turned. When he turned round again, something in his face had been put away.

“It wasn’t there.”

“No.”

“You’ve cracked it,” Dunmore said.

“Cracks wander. Show me a crack that holds a circle to a tenth of a millimeter.”

She measured it. A tenth of a millimeter at 15:10. Three tenths at 16:00. At 17:00 it was six, and anyone could see it, a dark thread with the end beyond it standing very slightly proud.

Natasha did not guess aloud. She went to the bench and worked for ten minutes with her back to the room, braced against the roll, and when she turned she had half a page.

“It isn’t solid. We have that from the weight. Say there’s liquid in it, and say it went in at depth. Water squeezes. Not much. A little under one percent at two hundred atmospheres.” She held up the page. “Bring it to the surface and the liquid wants its one percent back. If there’s twenty liters in there, it wants a hundred and eighty milliliters of room it hasn’t got. That end’s a piston. Nine centimeters radius. A hundred and eighty over the area is seven millimeters.”

“Why slowly?” Iyer said. “The pressure came off at noon.”

“Because the wall’s been stretched round it since then, holding the difference, and it’s letting go at its own rate.” Natasha glanced at Miriam. “It gives. You wrote that.”

“I wrote it about a millimeter.”

“It’s the same millimeter.”

“Say it plainly,” Carrow said.

“It’s opening. There’s no latch, because where it was, it didn’t need one. The sea was holding it shut. Two hundred atmospheres leaning on a lid. We took the sea away this morning.” She put the page down. She was a careful woman. “I’m not saying why it was there. I’m saying what the water did.”

“Pods do it,” Dunmore said. He had his arms folded. “Half the seed cases on earth open along a line when the conditions change. Nobody calls that a lid.”

“I’ll call it a line. For now.”

Carrow was looking at the long case, which stood against the bulkhead where he had lashed it.

“If it had gone straight into that,” he said.

“Then some time tonight the end would have come off in the dark, with nobody’s hands under it. Whatever’s inside would have gone into your foam.”

He took that in. Miriam had watched men in his trade be handed a fact that broke their plan, and had watched most of them argue with the fact. He did not.

“Then Dr. Iyer was right this morning,” he said, “for a reason none of us had.”

“I’ll take it,” Iyer said.

Miriam wrote: *No latch. The sea held it shut. Opens when the sea lets go. (AD: pods.)* She gave Dunmore his initials. He saw her do it and looked away, not displeased.

### 21:10.

By nine in the evening the gap was four millimeters, and the cap could be seen to shift when the ship rolled.

Iyer had sent everyone to eat in turns. She had eaten nothing herself. She stood at the foot of the tank with her glasses pushed up into her grey hair, and she said the most exact sentence anyone spoke that day.

“We are not opening it. It is opening. All I am deciding is whose hands are under the cap when it comes.”

“Mine,” Natasha said.

“Yours. Alistair, the lamp. Dr. Calder, the log, with times. Mr. Carrow may stand where he’s standing.”

“Thank you,” Carrow said.

Natasha put on long gloves and put her arms into four-degree water to above the elbow, and held them there. She cupped one hand under the cap and laid the other along the body. She did not pull. For forty minutes she did not do anything. Her lips went slowly pale. Nobody offered to relieve her, because nobody else’s hands had been there from the start.

At 21:52 the ship took one of its long lifts, and the cap came away into her palm.

Something came with it. For a second the water at the open end shivered, the way air shivers over a road, and a slow tongue of it slid out and down across the rack.

“Denser,” Natasha said through her teeth. “That’s not seawater.”

“Nothing is taken out of it,” Carrow said.

“It’s taking itself out. I can let it go into the tank, or I can have sixty milliliters of it in a syringe.”

Nothing came out of the cylinder. That was Carrow’s condition, and nobody needed to break it.

The outflow had drawn the free edge of the nearest roll a little way past the rim. It lay in the cold water like a tongue, a hand wide and as long as a finger, and it moved when the ship moved. Natasha swung the boom lens over it and put the picture on the wall screen. It went out of focus on the roll, and came back.

It was covered in small figures.

They ran in rows, nine rows on the strip, close and even. Each figure was a millimeter and a half high, darker than the sheet and denser. They were not laid on it. They were in it, the way grain is in wood.

For a while nobody spoke. Then Dunmore did, and what he said was good. Miriam thought so at the time.

“Before anyone says the word. The thing grew. We agree on that. Your own spectrum says so. Things that grow lay down pattern, and they do it without anybody’s help. There’s a banded agate on my desk at home with four hundred lines in it as even as ruled paper. Nobody ruled it. Salts came out of a gel in pulses, and there’s an equation for the spacing.” He pointed at the screen with his pen. “There’s a sea snail that draws triangles on its own shell. Thousands of them, nested. It looks like a page of somebody’s algebra. It’s one row of pigment cells, each switching on or off by what its neighbors did a minute ago. Two states and one rule. A child could write the rule.”

“A cone shell,” Natasha said.

“A cone shell. And this is a very good shell. I’ll say that for it. But rows are what growth makes. Rows are growth lines. You’ll have to do better than rows.”

“I agree,” Miriam said.

He had been braced for something else, and it showed.

“Rows are nothing. How long have I got?”

“The boat is at first light,” Carrow said. “A little before seven.”

“Then give me an hour of it to count.”

She took a stool to the screen, and a clean page, and did the first thing anyone in her trade does with marks they cannot read. She stopped looking at them, and counted.

It is dull work. She had always liked it. She went along each row and gave every figure she had not seen before a number, and every figure she had seen before the number it already had, and she drew each new one once in the margin, small. When the roll took the focus away she held her pencil on the place and waited for it. Natasha stayed at her shoulder for a time, and then went to hold a mug of tea in both hands. Iyer went to write the daily report. Carrow did not sit down. Dunmore took the other stool and watched her pencil. He said nothing for fifty minutes, and from him that was a courtesy.

“Two hundred and twelve places,” she said at last. “Thirty-one different figures.”

“Meaning?”

“Less than I’d like. A cone shell has two. This has at least thirty-one, and on a strip this small the real count is higher. I can’t tell you how much higher.” She turned the page toward him. “Here’s what I can say. They aren’t used equally. Figure two turns up twenty-seven times. Nine of them turn up once. And some runs repeat. This, then this, then this. The same three in a row, four times on the strip.”

“Crystals repeat.”

“Crystals repeat everything. This repeats some things and not others.” She stopped, and made herself say the rest. “And that isn’t proof either. You get the same lopsided count from a monkey at a keyboard. A few things often, most things rarely. It falls out of almost any process that strings pieces together. I’ve stood up in rooms full of admirals and told them so, when they wanted a signal to be a language and it was a pump.”

Dunmore looked at her for some time.

“You’re arguing my side.”

“I haven’t got a side. I’m telling you what won’t do.”

“It won’t do.”

They sat in front of the screen in something very like comfort. The ship climbed and settled. Carrow, behind them, had not written anything down for an hour.

It was Dunmore who found the tear. He had been looking at the whole strip while she had her nose in the rows, as a man reads a rock face before he takes out his lens. He pointed with the pen. “What’s that? Lower left. The pale line.”

It ran slantwise across three rows near the edge. It was a seam of slightly thicker stuff, like the ridge on a branch where a graft has taken. At some time the sheet had been torn there. It had closed. And across the seam, every figure the tear had cut was whole again. They were a little coarse along the join, as a healed thing is. But each was the same figure it had been, and each sat in its own place.

“There,” Dunmore said. He did not raise his voice. He did not need to. “It mends. It’s tissue. It was torn, and it put back what the pattern says goes there. That is what growth does, Calder. That’s all growth knows how to do.”

“Yes,” Miriam said. “I think that’s exactly right.”

She wrote it down in his words, with his initials.

“Alistair. You’ve got the lamp. Can you bring it right down? I want the light flat along the surface, from the left.”

“Raking light,” Dunmore said. He was already moving. “I’ve been raking crystal faces for forty years. You needn’t explain it.”

She had learned it at nineteen, from a man in Durham with a desk lamp and a tray of green bronze. When a surface has lost a thing, the shadows remember it. ### Year 305 of the Pilgrimage

The Reader’s book was heavier than Wren had expected, and it was longer.

She found that out by weight before she found it out by reading. Sister Perpetua put it into her two hands across the table in the catechist’s room. Wren had heard the Reading in the hub every year of her life and could have said most of it asleep, and she felt at once that there was too much of it.

“It’s the same Reading,” she said.

“It is the Reading. What you hear in the hub is what the hub can carry in an hour in the dark.” The sister folded her hands. “Open it at the ribbon.”

It was copied in a small old hand, brown on cream. Wren knew the place. In the hub it went: *And the Founder lifted the stone, and bore it up out of the deep. And the witness spoke of the warmth of the water. And she opened it.* Then the rings answered.

The book did not say that.

*And the stone was not lifted. It stood upon one foot, and she said, Do not raise it, but draw it out along its own bed. And they drew it out, and left the stone standing.*

*And the youth who shut the door bore it in his arms, eleven steps, and said, It is warm.*

*And the witness said, It is four degrees. Your hands are cold.*

*And no hand opened it. The sea had held it shut, and the sea let it go.*

Wren read it twice. Then she read the line before and the line after, to be sure she had not lost her place.

“She didn’t carry it.”

“No.”

“In the hub she carries it. And it’s Natasha who says it’s warm. Here she says—” Wren put her finger under the words. “She’s telling him he’s wrong.”

Sister Perpetua did not lean over to look. She had no need to. She was sixty-three, and she had stood at the last Opening as a young woman, and Wren had never once seen her check a line.

“The commentaries hold that the four degrees are the four stages by which a cold heart is warmed,” the sister said. “There is a hymn. You have sung it.”

“It sounds like a temperature.”

“Yes,” said Sister Perpetua. “It does.”

She let that stand between them for the length of a breath and a third.

“And nobody opened it,” Wren said. “Then what is it we open? At the Opening?”

“A box that somebody built to keep it in. That one has a latch.” The sister touched the edge of the table, and went on in her ordinary voice. “At the Vigil you will read what is in the Reading. In this room you will read what is in the book. A Reader knows which is which, and keeps them apart, and does not suppose that either one is a lie. That is the office. It is most of the office. The reliquary is opened this year. You will stand nearer to it than anyone your age has stood in thirty years. I thought you should know, before then, whose arms it was in.”

“Who was he? The youth.”

“He shut the door. The book does not give him another name.”

Wren looked at the page again. Eleven steps. Somebody had counted. Somebody who was not carrying it, and had nothing to do with her hands, had stood there and counted a boy’s steps and thought the number worth keeping.

She thought she knew who.

When the sister had gone, she stood up from the table and held out her arms, the way you would for a thing a meter long, and tried to feel the weight in them. She could not decide how heavy to pretend it was. The book did not say.
