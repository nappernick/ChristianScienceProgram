The Scrolls Beneath the Sea

Natasha was speaking to her.

Mariana realized this the way you realize rain has started — not at the first drop but several seconds in, when the pattern finally registers. She'd been watching the monitor above the prep station, half-reading the telemetry feed from one of the survey bots, half-thinking about nothing at all. Something about time and space. That might have been Natasha or it might have been her own mind drifting. Hard to tell at this hour.

"I lost the plot," she said. "Could you say all of that again?"

Without complaint, as Natasha was wont to do, she took Mariana back through each step of sequencing for the dive — who was going down in what order, what the bot had flagged overnight, how long they'd have at depth. Mariana had done enough of these to follow the shape of it. Her body knew what prep looked like. Her hands were already checking her equipment while Natasha talked.

"Am I this foggy because of the pressure chamber," she asked, "or is it doing strange things to my inner ear? I can't tell if I need a doctor or just coffee."

Natasha smiled but didn't answer, which was itself an answer. They'd developed a shorthand in the weeks they'd been together aboard, but before diving thousands of feet below the ocean, the implicit reassurance of silence would not have been her first choice.

Competent fellow scientists, a confounding problem to solve, and a beautiful ocean to look at. There were worse ways to spend a month.

But the problem had gotten more confounding overnight.

The indicators near the dig site were pushing the dating back further than anyone had expected. The numbers coming in from the geological team put the basalt surrounding the object — she didn't yet know what it was — at first one hundred, then two hundred thousand years old.

Mariana had heard the figure discussed at dinner the previous night with the particular energy that scientists generate when something doesn't make sense, or when they get the opportunity to call out a peer for idiocy — not excitement exactly, more like the agitation of a system encountering an input it can't process.

Two hundred thousand years put it well before the first hominid ever walked the earth. The geological team had run the argon-argon dating multiple times, because the certainty you need to present a number that overturns the current models of human evolution is, well, very certain. You don't say that casually. The most likely explanation was cross-contamination of the silts surrounding the site, mineral intrusion from an older stratum that skewed the isotope ratios. That happened. It was mundane and fixable. But they'd run the analysis again and again and kept getting the same number. Two hundred thousand years.

Either way — surrounded by fools or on the brink of world-changing discovery — she was excited to see something older than any empire, at the least.

Something about the sonar profile had been bothering her for days. Not the dating — that was the geologists' problem and they were welcome to it. Something about the contact layer beneath the basalt. The surface the object appeared to be resting on. Her archaeological training made her read depositional context the way other people read faces — automatically, without deciding to. You don't date a shipwreck by dating the seafloor around it. You date the ship. You ask when the object arrived, not when the environment formed. And the relationship between this object and the stratum beneath the basalt didn't sit right. She'd made a note of it in her notebook, a half-formed question she hadn't shown anyone, and left it at that.

She'd learned when to share and when to wait from Dr. Locke, though he would have hated knowing it. Her undergraduate archaeology professor, a man who had made an unnecessarily clear show of disliking her for reasons she'd never fully understood. And yet his name had appeared on the recommendation that put her on the team recovering the Antikythera mechanism from the bottom of the Mediterranean — humanity's oldest known computer, two thousand years old, pulled from the remains of a Roman merchant ship that had the great fortune of sinking before it could deliver the device to someone who would have lost it. The crowning achievement of quite literally any archaeologist, and she'd been twenty-six.

That recovery had been the defining experience of her early career. Not because of what they found but because of what it taught her about patience, about the gap between what you think you're looking for and what's actually there, about the particular silence that falls over a team when something comes up from the water that changes what you thought you knew.

She hadn't heard from Locke since. Until three weeks ago, when his name appeared again on the communication requesting her for this expedition. A geological survey in the North Atlantic that needed a cultural analyst. Routine. Unglamorous. Except the site had started producing readings that made routine geologists argue at dinner.

She finished her prep check and looked at Natasha.

"Ready when you are."

It was time to go down.
